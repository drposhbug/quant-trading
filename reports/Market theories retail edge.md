# Rival market theories agree on structural edges

The ten main theories of how the stock market works disagree about *why* prices move. On what a small trader can still harvest they mostly agree: almost nothing that requires out-predicting the market in megacap tech, and a few things that come from rules institutions must obey and a taxable individual need not. Twenty years of replication evidence settles the efficient-versus-behavioral fight as a draw. Mispricing is real, but published anomalies lose about **58% of their returns after publication** ([CFA Digest on McLean-Pontiff](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2016/06/does-academic-research-destroy-stock-return-predictability-digest-summary)), and after trading costs the average one nets about **4 bp a month** ([Chen & Velikov, JFQA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/zeroing-in-on-the-expected-returns-of-anomalies/945133D5A3ECEEAF466AEE91551FD225)). What survives sits in small, illiquid, hard-to-short stocks, the opposite of a megacap tech book. Macro-liquidity timing, crash prediction from complexity science, chart patterns and narrative intensity all fail out-of-sample tests at retail horizons. Flows do move prices, but the predictable ones get front-run. The frozen benchmark (long-only inverse-vol tech/AI megacaps, 20% vol target, exposure halved below QQQ's 200-day average, T-bills for idle cash) already holds the two ideas that survive best: trend as drawdown insurance and volatility scaling as risk control. It also avoids the behavior gap that costs ordinary investors about **1.2 pp a year** ([Morningstar 2026](https://www.morningstar.com/content/cs-assets/v3/assets/blt9415ea4cc4157833/blt852a4301adc2f5db/6a7373d51f7b5ae2da78e1e8/US_Mind_the_Gap_2026.pdf)). It leaves two openings. It trades as if it were tax-exempt, and in crashes it de-risks like a constrained institution, at the moment intermediary theory says expected returns are highest. Ranked by my own subjective, uncalibrated estimate of surviving a pre-registered test, the best candidates are **tax-aware execution in a taxable account (about 40%)**, a muni cash sleeve and month-end pension-rebalancing timing (about 15% each), and faster re-entry after VIX≥40 panics (about 10%). The 52-week-high, Lazy Prices and 4-week net-liquidity ideas each sit at 5% or below. Grok and Astra, prompted independently, put the same tax idea first and rated nothing above 35%. Their eight-plus-eight theories are hypotheses, not evidence, and are labelled as such throughout.

## Ten theories, one market: where the camps fight and what settles it

Each theory makes a different claim about where returns come from, and that decides what a retail trader should expect. The **efficient markets hypothesis** says prices reflect available information, so returns are compensation for systematic risk and every deviation from the market is a bet that must justify itself ([Fama 1970](https://doi.org/10.2307/2325486)). Its strongest support is that most active money loses. Over 15 years no category of active managers has a majority beating its benchmark ([SPIVA YE2024](https://spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-us-year-end-2024)), and the most active fifth of 66,465 households earned **11.4% a year against 17.9%** for the market ([Barber & Odean](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/returns/returns.html)). **Behavioral finance** answers that psychology causes systematic mispricing. Its evidence includes Shiller's excess volatility ([AER 1981](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1802789)), momentum, and underreaction effects such as "Lazy Prices," where firms that quietly change their 10-K language underperform by up to **188 bp a month**, with no announcement-day reaction and no reversal over 18 months ([NBER w25084](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25084)). **Limits to arbitrage** explains why mispricing survives: arbitrageurs invest other people's money and get redeemed exactly when spreads widen ([Shleifer & Vishny 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03807.x)). The **Grossman-Stiglitz paradox** adds that markets cannot be fully efficient, or nobody would pay to make them so. The equilibrium leaves just enough inefficiency to pay the cheapest information-gatherer ([AER 1980](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1805228)). **Adaptive markets** treats efficiency as varying over time: edges appear, get competed away and return when the investor population changes ([Lo 2004](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/1721.1/75362/1/Lo_Adaptive%20Markets.pdf)).

The **factor and risk-premia** camp holds that persistent returns pay for risks others dislike. Low-beta assets beat high-beta ones because leverage-constrained investors bid up beta ([Frazzini & Pedersen](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/60082899/lasse_heje_pedersen_et_al_betting_against_beta_publishersversion.pdf)). Intermediary asset pricing finds that shocks to dealers' capital carry a positive risk price across stocks, bonds, options, CDS and commodities, so premia spike when dealers are capital-poor ([He, Kelly & Manela](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21920.pdf)). **Macro-liquidity** says the Fed, money and credit drive prices. **Flows and inelastic markets** say money moving in and out moves prices far more than efficient-market models allow: Gabaix and Koijen estimate that **$1 into equities raises aggregate value by about $5** ([NBER w28967](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28967/w28967.pdf)). **Complexity and econophysics** treat markets as adaptive systems with fat tails and endogenous crashes. **Technical analysis and narrative economics** hold that price patterns and viral stories carry tradable information. **Reflexivity** (Soros) says beliefs change fundamentals, since a high share price buys cheap capital that funds real growth. It is arguably the most relevant theory for an AI capex boom, and the least tested: the research found no formal academic test of it.

The synthesis that fits all of this is Grossman-Stiglitz plus limits to arbitrage, with adaptive-market dynamics on top. An equilibrium amount of inefficiency survives where arbitrage is expensive, and it shrinks as costs fall and knowledge spreads. That implies a hierarchy for the frozen benchmark. Megacap tech is the most covered, most liquid, most NLP-scraped segment on earth, so information-based alpha should be close to zero there. The repo agrees: **0 of 44** macro tests, 0 of 9 event and calendar tests, 0 of 23 alternative-data tests and 0 of 39 candlestick tests survived correction ([MATH.md](../MATH.md)). Gross alpha accrues to whoever has the lowest cost of information and execution, and a retail account has neither advantage. Its structural differences are tax status, horizon and freedom from redemptions, and freedom from a benchmark. The surviving factor themes (value, momentum, quality, low risk) persist at roughly half their published size and need multi-year patience. Of those, the benchmark holds momentum through its 200-day filter and is structurally short value.

Two theories deserve a sharper verdict than "mixed." **Macro-liquidity** loses as a forecasting claim. The Fed's effect on stocks is "large, even for average stock returns earned over periods of several decades," but it arrives through policy *surprises*, and even the famous Fed-calendar anomalies decayed. The pre-FOMC drift fell from **49 bp to about 20 bp per meeting** after publication, and the FOMC-cycle effect turned negative after 2016 ([Knox & Vissing-Jorgensen, FEDS 2026-023](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026023pap.pdf)). Its best-known practitioner forecasters miss too. Howell expected a further ~20% correction in 2023 ([Roger Montgomery](https://rogermontgomery.com/crossborder-capitals-liquidity-warning/)), and the S&P rose 24% that year ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/economy/indicator-foretold-almost-every-recession-doesnt-seem-working-anymore-rcna163522)). **Narrative economics** wins on explanation and loses on prediction. A topic model of 763,887 WSJ articles explains about 25% of monthly market returns, but only in the *same* month, falling to 17.8% out of sample. The authors say plainly that contemporaneous fit "has little to say" about incremental information ([Bybee, Kelly, Manela & Xiu](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29344/w29344.pdf)). The one quantified crash-narrative result points the other way from what bears expect: investors put the odds of a 1929- or 1987-style crash in the next six months at about **10% versus a historical 1.7%** ([Goetzmann, Kim & Shiller](https://www.nber.org/digest/jul16/w22143.html)). Crowds overprice crash fear. They do not forecast crashes.

Complexity science contributes the most robust single fact in the whole debate and no trading signal. Return tails follow a power law with exponent near 3, so variance exists but Gaussian risk models understate single-day ruin ([Gopikrishnan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9803374)). Volatility has long memory, with autocorrelation in absolute returns out to about 2,700 days ([Lobato & Savin](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/em/papers/9605/9605004.html)). Direction does not. The Santa Fe artificial market shows bubbles and crashes emerging endogenously once agents learn fast enough ([Arthur et al.](https://sites.santafe.edu/~wbarthur/Papers/Artif_Mkt.pdf)). It gives theoretical cover for reflexivity but no forecasting content. The practical use is the one the benchmark already makes: forecast volatility and scale exposure to it.

## In bad times correlations converge, recoveries take years, and the best days hide in the worst months

Crises have four regular features, and each bears on the benchmark. Volatility clusters and rises more after falls than after rises. Bekaert and Wu favor the volatility-feedback story, in which higher expected volatility raises required returns and pushes prices down ([NBER W6022](https://www.nber.org/papers/W6022)). Correlations rise in bear markets but not in bull markets ([Longin & Solnik](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:56:y:2001:i:2:p:649-676)), so a basket of a dozen AI names behaves like one asset in a crash. Liquidity spirals make declines accelerate: falling prices raise margins and dealer losses shrink liquidity provision, which can dry up suddenly because the model has multiple equilibria ([Brunnermeier & Pedersen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12939.pdf)). Forced sellers then create the overshoot behind post-crash rebounds. An unlevered account sits outside that spiral, which is a real structural advantage, provided it does not mechanically sell alongside the forced sellers.

Tech bears are deeper and longer than broad-market bears. Since 1929 the average bear market has fallen **33.5% over 9.6 months** ([Kiplinger/Ned Davis](https://www.kiplinger.com/slideshow/investing/t052-s001-8-facts-you-need-to-know-about-bear-markets/index.html)). The Nasdaq-100 lost **36.1%, 33.3% and 37.4%** in 2000, 2001 and 2002, **41.7%** in 2008 and **32.4%** in 2022 ([A Wealth of Common Sense](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2025/12/the-nasdaq-100-booms-busts/)). The Nasdaq Composite fell **78%** peak to trough ([DQYDJ](https://dqydj.com/nasdaq-drawdown-history/)) and did not regain its 2000 price high until about 2015. Leaders fare worse. Railway shares fell 64% after 1845 ([Campbell, VoxEU](https://cepr.org/article/railway-mania-not-so-great-expectations)), and RCA fell about 98% by 1932 ([Global Financial Data](https://www.globalfinancialdata.com/rca-and-the-roaring-twenties)). Cisco, the world's largest company in March 2000, needed **more than 25 years** to regain its peak even as revenue more than quadrupled ([Nasdaq.com](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/25-years-later-cisco-finally-recovers-dot-com-crash)). The Nifty Fifty is the gentler precedent. Bought at the 1972 peak, the group returned **12.5% a year against 12.7%** for the S&P over 26 years, and its cheaper half did about twice as well as its dearest half ([Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2024/02/21/magnificent-seven-bubble-nifty-fifty)). Run on the Nasdaq-100 since 1985, the benchmark's engine turned a −77% fall in 2000–02 into −41% and a −83% maximum drawdown into −39%, at a higher Sharpe (0.78 vs 0.63) and a lower CAGR (11.8% vs 14.1%) ([MATH.md](../MATH.md)). That is the trade the frozen model makes, and it is the right frame for every challenger below.

The late-2026 market sits where historians start to worry, though that tells them nothing about timing. CAPE is about 40, second only to 2000's 43.5 ([GMO](https://www.gmo.com/americas/research-library/valuing-ai-extreme-bubble-new-golden-era-or-both/)). FINRA margin debt reached a record **$1.50 trillion** in June 2026, up 51.5% year on year ([Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/dshort/updates/2026/06/24/margin-debt-finra)). The top ten stocks hit a record **40.7%** of the S&P 500 against about 27% in 2000 ([RBC](https://www.rbcwealthmanagement.com/en-us/insights/the-great-narrowing-sp-500-concentration)). Hyperscaler capex is about 1.3% of GDP, already past the telecom peak of 1.2%, against estimated AI revenue under $50B ([GMO](https://www.gmo.com/americas/research-library/valuing-ai-extreme-bubble-new-golden-era-or-both/)). On 5 November 2026 an AI-led selloff took about **6%** off the Nasdaq-100 in a day, with AMD down 15–17% ([Korea Times/Reuters](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/world/20261106/gathering-ai-storm-clouds-doom-tech-stocks-to-darkest-day-in-a-year)). The best evidence on timing is probabilistic. An industry that beats the market by 100% over two years has about a **53% chance** of a 40% crash within two years, about 80% at +150%. Run-ups alone do not predict lower *average* returns, because some industries never crash ([Greenwood, Shleifer & You](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23191/w23191.pdf)). Goldman found that after most of seven historical concentration peaks the market *kept rallying* ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/is-the-sp-too-concentrated)), though that comes from a search snippet of a blocked page and another snippet of the same article reads the other way. GMO has called the US a bubble since 2021. In January 2026 it conceded that the usual top signals, with speculative names collapsing and quality outperforming, were "not yet clearly visible" ([GMO](https://www.gmo.com/americas/research-library/valuing-ai-extreme-bubble-new-golden-era-or-both/)). Concentration predicts relative returns over a decade, not index tops: the largest stock in a sector has lagged its sector by about **3.3% a year** over the next ten years ([Research Affiliates via ioandc](https://ioandc.com/rob-arnott-sell-the-top-dogs)).

Recoveries are where the theories collide most usefully. Trend filters protect against slow bears and pay for it in whipsaws. The S&P crossed its 200-day average about **180 times** from 1994 to 2019, and a 5-day smoothing cut the bullish signals from 90 to 34 ([StockCharts](https://stockcharts.com/articles/arthurhill/2019/02/just-how-effective-is-the-200-day-moving-average.html)). After a financial crisis *begins*, trend returns average less than half their normal level for about four years ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/?p=34042)). "You'll miss the best days" is mostly arithmetic. Missing the best 10 days from 1997 to 2024 cost 3.1 pp a year, missing the worst 10 *added* 3.6 pp, and realistic great-versus-terrible timing in an 80/20 portfolio is worth only about ±1.6–1.8 pp ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/insights/perspectives/so-what-if-you-miss-the-markets-n-best-days)). The best days cluster where a filter is out. About 78% of the S&P's best days in the last 20 years came in bear markets or the first two months of a bull market ([Hartford Funds](https://www.hartfordfunds.com/practice-management/client-conversations/managing-volatility/timing-the-market-is-impossible.html)).

Buying into extreme fear has positive expectancy and large variance. Since 1990 the VIX closed at or above 40 on 208 days, but those days form only **9 independent episodes**. The S&P was higher 12 months later about 96% of the time, but only about 67% of the time 3 months later, *below* the calm-market base rate ([ChartingLens](https://chartinglens.com/blog/vix-levels-stock-market-returns)). Buying at the first 20% drawdown is close to a coin flip. Since 1906 the S&P was up a year later only **55%** of the time, with an average loss of −21% when it was down ([Yahoo Finance/Fidelity](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/probability-another-20-drawdown-higher-204027742.html)). Momentum crashes in exactly these "panic states," after declines and while volatility is high, because past losers behave like calls in the rebound ([Daniel & Moskowitz](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439)). Intermediary theory supplies the mechanism: risk premia are highest when dealers' capital is depleted ([He, Kelly & Manela](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21920.pdf)). The benchmark's two brakes, high realized volatility and price below the 200-day average, are both on hardest at that moment. That is the tension behind hypothesis H5 below.

## Psychology moves prices where arbitrage is weakest, and retail has turned from panic seller to dip buyer

Biases leave measurable footprints, but mostly in places this benchmark does not trade. Robinhood herding into the day's most-bought stocks preceded a **−4.7% 20-day abnormal return**, driven partly by the app's top-mover lists ([Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz, JF 2022](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/attention-induced-trading-and-returns-evidence-from-robinhood-use/)). Lottery-like stocks with a large maximum daily return underperformed by more than 1% a month ([Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:99:y:2011:i:2:p:427-446)), but the effect is concentrated before the 1990s and largely proxies for idiosyncratic volatility ([Springer MRQ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11301-019-00176-3)). The disposition effect, selling winners too early and holding losers, creates a capital-gains overhang that subsumes momentum in-sample ([Grinblatt & Han](https://nber.org/papers/w8734)). Anchoring on the 52-week high explains much of momentum's profit, with an original spread of about **0.45% a month** that depends on sentiment ([George & Hwang via Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/the-secret-to-momentum-is-the-52-week-high/)). Underreaction to earnings is gone in large stocks: post-earnings drift "has been non-existent since 2006" for them ([Martineau, CFR 2022](https://cfr.ivo-welch.info/published/papers/martineau2021rest.pdf)), though this is disputed ([UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/is-post-earnings-announcement-drift-a-thing-again)). Baker-Wurgler sentiment indices generally fail to predict returns out of sample ([AEA 2026](https://benny.aeaweb.org/conference/2026/program/paper/6Z8QBKFb)). The January effect, once above 2.5%, was small or absent from 2000 to 2023 ([NC State](https://mba.ncsu.edu/2024/12/17/does-the-stock-market-feel-the-holiday-spirit)). Even price-level psychology works against the believers. Retail liquidity takers overtrade around round-number prices and lose close to **$1B a year** doing it ([Bhattacharya, Holden & Jacobsen](https://papers.ssrn.com/ab_id=1569922)).

The pattern is consistent across camps. Psychology-driven mispricing survives where arbitrage is limited (small, illiquid, hard-to-short stocks) and decays where it is not, and the US shows the clearest post-publication decline, about **62–66%** ([Jacobs & Müller](https://www.wiwi.hu-berlin.de/en/Professorships/bwl/finance/seminars/papers-fin-acc-res-seminars/17s/jacobs.pdf)). For a long-only megacap book, the usable behavioral edge is defensive: do not be the attention buyer, the lottery buyer or the short-dated option buyer. Retail options trade at an average bid-ask spread of **12.6%** and lose money on average ([Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya, JF 2023](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/2827/1/The%20Journal%20of%20Finance%20-%202023%20-%20BRYZGALOVA%20-%20Retail%20Trading%20in%20Options%20and%20the%20Rise%20of%20the%20Big%20Three%20Wholesalers.pdf)).

One stereotype no longer fits the market this benchmark trades. Retail did not panic in the 2025 tariff selloff. On 3 April 2025 individuals net bought **$4.7bn** of US equities, the largest daily inflow in about a decade ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/15/stocks-tariffs-tesla-nvidia-palantir)). In the first half of 2026 retail bought about **3.5 times** its daily average on S&P down days, "the strongest buy-the-dip behavior in our dataset" ([Citadel Securities](https://www.citadelsecurities.com/?p=43645)). The crowd was trained by V-shaped recoveries, it is concentrated in NVDA and TSLA, and it is partly leveraged. While recoveries keep coming it acts as a support layer, and if a 2000–02-style regime arrives in which dips keep falling it becomes a correlated risk. Either way it is a risk input, not a signal. The data are proprietary press-release aggregates that cannot be backtested.

The behavioral effect with the deepest theory is one the benchmark already collects. Loss-averse investors who check their portfolios often demand a large equity premium, and annual evaluation fits the observed premium ([Benartzi & Thaler, QJE 1995](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:110:y:1995:i:1:p:73-92.)). The premium partly pays people to sit through losses that myopic investors will not. The operator's own behavior is the larger threat. Fund investors lag their own funds by about **1.2 pp a year**: **0.4 pp in the least volatile funds and 2.1 pp in the most volatile** ([Morningstar 2026](https://www.morningstar.com/content/cs-assets/v3/assets/blt9415ea4cc4157833/blt852a4301adc2f5db/6a7373d51f7b5ae2da78e1e8/US_Mind_the_Gap_2026.pdf)). A concentrated 20%-volatility tech book sits in the high-gap bucket. Any challenger that adds discretion, or adds rules that tempt mid-drawdown tinkering, should be charged for that risk before it earns a single basis point.

## Two openings: the benchmark pays tax like an endowment and de-risks like a fund

Read through the theories, the frozen benchmark has a clear profile. Trend filters and volatility scaling are the survivors of the technical, complexity and volatility literatures. Mechanical rules close the behavior gap. Inverse-volatility weighting is the risk-based construction that holds up best when expected returns are unknowable. Every prediction-based attack on it runs into Grossman-Stiglitz, because megacap tech is where information is cheapest for everyone else. The challengers that still have a mechanism exploit a constraint the benchmark obeys and the trader does not.

The first constraint is **tax status**. Pensions and endowments are mostly tax-exempt, so tax alpha is an edge that exists only for taxable individuals. Tax-loss harvesting on the CRSP top 500 from 1926 to 2018 was worth about **1.08% a year**, falling to **0.82%** under wash-sale rules, and it varied by era, from 2.13% in 1926–49 to 0.51% in 1949–72 ([Chaudhuri, Burnham & Lo, FAJ 2020](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2020/empirical-evaluation-tax-loss-harvesting-alpha)). The benefit depends on the investor having gains to offset ([AQR, Sosner et al.](https://www.aqr.com:443/-/media/AQR/Documents/Journal-Articles/AQR-The-Tax-Benefits-of-Direct-Indexing.pdf?sc_lang=en)). The benchmark rebalances daily on noisy 60-day volatility estimates, which in a taxable account realizes short-term gains at ordinary rates on trades that barely change risk. It also generates **wash sales against itself**, because its routine daily buys of a name fall within 30 days of any loss sale in that name. The second constraint is **career and redemption risk**. The 200-day halving and the volatility target sell after vol spikes and price breaks, as a redemption-exposed fund must. A trader with no clients can hold through, and the intermediary evidence says that is when the premium is richest. Trend's century of crisis protection says the brake is worth having, so the question is not whether to remove it but whether to release it faster once panic has peaked.

## Fourteen hypotheses, ranked, with exact pre-registered tests

### Evidence versus model output

Two sources feed the list, and they must not be confused. **Evidence-derived** hypotheses come from published research or from computations in this research (the net-liquidity rule). **Model-generated** hypotheses come from Grok (grok CLI, headless, no tools or web) and Astra (codex `gpt-6.2-astra`, read-only). Both answered the same prompt on 2026-11-20 and then critiqued each other once ([grok_astra_theories.md](../research_notes/Market%20theories%20retail%20edge/grok_astra_theories.md)). Nothing they proposed was backtested, and their probabilities are their own uncalibrated guesses. They agreed on more than expected. Both put tax-aware execution first. Both said bluntly that nothing is likely to work and that in an IRA the benchmark should be left alone. Each critic found every one of the other's proposals to be a known idea in new packaging. Their critiques caught real errors. FLOT holds investment-grade floating-rate notes, so it is the wrong instrument for a leveraged-loan fire-sale idea. Wash-sale rules also bite on purchases made *before* a loss sale. A VIX-based Black-Scholes price is a poor proxy for QQQ put premiums. Astra's three "trade less" ideas are one claim counted three times. Both critics flagged signal-at-close, fill-at-same-close look-ahead, and hindsight in the ticker list. The probabilities in my ranking are my own subjective estimates. They lean on this repo's prior results, which the models did not see.

### Shared protocol for every test

Every test below changes exactly one thing in the frozen model and inherits the repo's change-control rules ([MATH.md](../MATH.md)), tightened by the critiques above. The repo's own pre-registered equal-weight challenger was redefined for the same reason, after both models noted that the earlier version changed four things at once.

### H1. Tax-aware execution (rank 1, ~40% in a taxable account)

The mechanism is accounting, not forecasting, so other people doing it does not close it. Two investors holding identical portfolios face different optimal trades, because the value lives in the age and basis of each lot. It is known practice: the Constantinides tax-timing option, direct indexing and rebalancing bands. Both models' critiques made that point ([grok_astra_theories.md](../research_notes/Market%20theories%20retail%20edge/grok_astra_theories.md)). That is a reason to believe it works, not a reason to skip it, because the benchmark does not do it. A first-principles sizing supports a wide band. The optimal no-trade half-width scales with the cube root of the trading cost, about ±1.3 pp at 5 bp for an 8% weight ([Janeček & Shreve](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00780-003-0118-4), as derived in the prior report). Selling a lot with a 30% short-term gain at a 40.8% rate costs about 12% of the trade value, about 240 times 5 bp. The cube root of 240 is about 6.2, which puts the band near ±8 pp for those trades. That is far wider than the current ±1 pp. Two things limit the gain, and Grok named the main one. Much of the benchmark's turnover comes from aggregate exposure changes (vol target and 200-day halving), which this rule still executes, and in the first year every lot is short-term. Harvests are also front-loaded, because the basis ratchets down without new contributions.

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Ledger (all arms, including benchmark) | Lot-level US rules: short-term ≤ 365 days; wash sale on substantially identical purchases within 30 days **before or after** a loss sale, including the model's own routine buys. Default rates 40.8% short-term and ordinary, 23.8% long-term and qualified (top federal + NIIT, no state), plus a run at the user's actual bracket. T-bill interest is federally taxed and state-exempt. Losses net by character, $3,000 ordinary offset, carryforward. Tax paid from the account on the last session of each year. Start from cash |
| Benchmark arm | Frozen model's trades, FIFO lots |
| Challenger (one judged arm) | (a) Lot order on any sale: short-term losses, long-term losses, long-term gains (highest basis first), short-term gains (highest basis first). (b) Per-name band: a trade that would realize a net short-term gain happens only if the name is more than **5 pp** from target; other trades keep the ±1 pp band. (c) Aggregate exposure changes (vol target, 200-day) execute at the frozen target the same session. If they would sell a gain lot aged 335–365 days and no other lot can fill the sale, defer up to 30 sessions while the exposure overshoot stays ≤ 3 pp of NAV. (d) Harvest: any lot down ≥ 10% from basis is sold. Proceeds go to QQQ (if a single name) or to a different-index tech ETF such as VGT (if QQQ; never QQQM) for 31 days, then return to target unless the swap-back realizes a short-term gain above 0.5% of NAV |
| Controls | Placebo arm protecting lots aged 275–304 days instead of 335–365 (Astra). Frictionless rerun with all tax rates at zero, where the advantage must vanish or turn negative. Secondary report: seeded with lots bought two years before the window |
| Primary metric and pass | After-tax liquidation CAGR (everything sold and taxed at the final close). P(challenger ≤ benchmark) < 0.05 on both universes; pre-tax tracking error ≤ 2%/yr; challenger beats placebo |
| Expected effect | +0.2 to +0.8 pp a year after tax, front-loaded. Grok guessed +0.4 to +1.0 from cash and Astra +0.05 to +0.35 |
| Most likely failure | Exposure cuts in the first two years realize short-term gains regardless, and a 5 pp band leaves the book heavy in a name that then gaps down |

In an IRA none of this applies. What remains is the pre-tax residue of a wider band, which my estimate puts at about 5% to pass and which could easily be negative.

### H2. Muni idle cash in a taxable account (rank 2, ~15%; model-generated)

Grok's idea is that the benchmark's idle sleeve, often a quarter to half of NAV, earns a fully taxable T-bill yield, and a short national muni fund would keep more of it after tax ([grok_astra_theories.md](../research_notes/Market%20theories%20retail%20edge/grok_astra_theories.md)). Grok's protocol missed something that cuts against it: T-bill interest is exempt from state income tax, while out-of-state muni interest is not, so the edge shrinks in high-tax states. Astra's critique adds that deducting the expense ratio from ETF prices double-counts the fee, and that a duration-matched Treasury control is needed to separate the tax effect from a duration bet. Specify it as follows. Hold SUB in place of SGOV for every dollar the benchmark allocates to cash. Use actual SUB and SGOV distributions from the price history, with no extra fee deduction. Tax SGOV income at federal ordinary rates with no state tax. Tax SUB distributions federally exempt but state-taxable at the user's rate. Run a third arm holding a 1–3 year Treasury ETF to control for duration. The primary metric is after-tax liquidation CAGR of the whole portfolio, and it passes if P < 0.05 on both universes and SUB also beats the duration-matched control. Expected gain is +0.2 to +0.5 pp a year at the top federal bracket with no state tax. The likeliest failure is a 2022-style rate shock or a 2020-style muni liquidity break marking down "cash" when it is supposed to be ballast. Much of this can be checked from published yields before any backtest. In an IRA it is worth nothing.

### H3. Month-end pension-rebalancing timing (rank 3, ~15%)

This is the best-documented live flow effect. Balanced funds and pensions that drift overweight equities sell stocks and buy bonds. A 1σ rise in the calendar signal predicts a **−17 bp** next-day S&P return. Predictability peaks in the last four trading days of the month, and funds avoid the very last day. A managed portfolio on the signals earned Sharpe above 1 from 1997 to 2023, close to 1 after costs, and held up in the first half of the sample alone ([Harvey, Mazzoleni & Melone](https://www.edhec.edu/sites/default/files/2026-03/Scientific%20paper.%20ssrn-5122748.pdf)). It is consistent with the separate "dash for cash" before month-end ([Etula et al., RFS 2020](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/33/1/75/5494694)). Three priors push the other way. The effect was published in 2025–26, and the index-inclusion effect shows what front-running does to predictable flows ([Greenwood & Sammon](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30748/w30748.pdf)). One study finds no consistent calendar effects in the S&P 500 from 1988 to 2023 ([Patel via Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/calendar-anomalies/)). The repo's own turn-of-month test failed among 0 of 9 event tests ([MATH.md](../MATH.md)).

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Data | SPY and IEF adjusted closes |
| Signal | From each month-end close, drift a 60/40 portfolio: w = 0.6·G_SPY / (0.6·G_SPY + 0.4·G_IEF), where G is the gross return since that close. Deviation d = w − 0.60. Standardize d at the 5th-to-last trading day by its trailing 36-month standard deviation measured on the same day of each month, giving z |
| Rule (single change) | Using z from close T−5, at close T−4: if z ≥ +1, equity exposure becomes 0.8 × frozen exposure; if z ≤ −1, it becomes min(1, 1.2 × frozen exposure). Restore at close T−1, so the overlay covers the returns of T−3, T−2 and T−1 |
| Pass | Sharpe, P < 0.05 on both universes over the full window. Because 2015–2023 overlaps the paper's sample, shipping also needs a positive point estimate on 2023-03 onward, the first period after the paper's data end |
| Expected effect | +0.1 to +0.3 pp a year after a decay haircut of about 58% |
| Most likely failure | Front-running has already absorbed the pressure, and the overlay is too small relative to the book's 20% volatility to clear the bootstrap |

### H4. A hysteresis band on the 200-day halving (rank 4, ~10%; proposed here)

This is not a new signal. It is a turnover fix for an existing one, and it pulls in the opposite direction from H5, which is useful information either way. A bare 200-day crossing whipsaws often: about 180 S&P crossings in 25 years, cut to 34 bullish signals by light smoothing ([StockCharts](https://stockcharts.com/articles/arthurhill/2019/02/just-how-effective-is-the-200-day-moving-average.html)). Bands of about ±5% cut whipsaw and turnover ([Systematic Investor](https://systematicinvestor.github.io/Moving-Average)). The rule halves exposure only when QQQ closes more than 2% below its 200-day SMA, and restores it only when QQQ closes more than 2% above. Everything else stays frozen. The primary metric is Sharpe, passing at P < 0.05 on both universes plus ^NDX 1990–2026. In a taxable run, also report after-tax CAGR under the H1 ledger. The likeliest failure is that the band delays re-entry in V-shaped recoveries such as 2020 and 2025 by just the margin that matters, and that whipsaw costs at 5 bp are already too small to rescue.

### H5. Faster re-entry after VIX≥40 panics (rank 5, ~10%; model-generated, evidence-supported)

This is the cleanest theory-driven attack on the benchmark. The intermediary and limits-to-arbitrage camps say the post-crisis premium is highest when constrained capital is forced out, and the benchmark's brakes are fully on at that moment. The evidence for the premium is real but rests on about nine episodes since 1990, with poor 3-month odds ([ChartingLens](https://chartinglens.com/blog/vix-levels-stock-market-returns)). After VIX>30 crossings, 2008 still returned −10.8% over 12 months ([eco3min](https://eco3min.fr/en/vix-contrarian-buy-signal-historical-thresholds-equity-rebound/)). The repo's VIX tests failed at 1- and 5-day horizons ([macro_results.md](macro_results.md)). Those horizons are not the 6-month horizon tested here. Grok's version also removes the volatility target and sets exposure to 1, which changes several things at once and gives hindsight room in its 22/35 thresholds (Astra's critique). The isolated version below lifts only the 200-day halving.

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Data | FRED `VIXCLS`; QQQ or ^NDX closes |
| Episode start | First close at which the maximum `VIXCLS` over the prior 63 sessions is ≥ 40 **and** today's `VIXCLS` ≤ 0.6 × that maximum, with no episode started in the prior 126 sessions |
| Rule (single change) | From the next close, switch off the 200-day halving for 126 sessions or until QQQ closes above its 200-day SMA, whichever comes first. Volatility target, weights and caps unchanged |
| Secondary (reported, not judged) | Grok's rule: exposure set to 1 when `VIXCLS` ≤ 22 after a 126-session maximum ≥ 35 |
| Power rule | At least 6 qualifying episodes on ^NDX 1990–2026, otherwise "untested." Publish a per-episode table of P&L against the benchmark |
| Pass | Sharpe, P < 0.05 on ^NDX 1990–2026 and on both universes, with maximum drawdown no more than 3 pp worse than the benchmark |
| Expected effect | Zero in calm windows; +0.1 to +0.5 pp a year in windows containing a recovering panic |
| Most likely failure | A 2008-style second leg down after volatility first eases. One false start can erase a decade of gains, and five forward years may contain a single episode |

### H6. An AI-hardware cluster cap, tested against 2000–02 and 2022 (rank 6, ~8%)

The concentration case is historically strong for relative returns and weak for timing. Top dogs lag by about 3% a year over a decade ([Research Affiliates via ioandc](https://ioandc.com/rob-arnott-sell-the-top-dogs)). The dearest Nifty Fifty names returned half as much as the cheapest ([Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2024/02/21/magnificent-seven-bubble-nifty-fifty)). Two of 2025–26's sharpest losses at sophisticated funds came from the crowded AI-hardware trade, which hedge funds held at the 98th percentile of semis net exposure in mid-2026 (prior report, [Top traders methods and math.md](Top%20traders%20methods%20and%20math.md)). Equal weighting, Grok's version, has already been tested and closed: it lost on the end-2014 list ([MATH.md](../MATH.md)). The remaining test is a cap on the semiconductor cluster. Inverse-volatility weighting already underweights volatile semis, so the cap may rarely bind. Measure the binding frequency first, and if the cap binds on fewer than 10% of days, record the test as moot without running it.

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Rule (single change) | Combined weight of GICS semiconductor and semiconductor-equipment names in the equity sleeve ≤ 40%. The excess goes to non-cluster names pro rata to their inverse-vol weights. Vol target, 200-day rule and caps unchanged |
| Universes | Shipped and end-2014 lists (repo rule). For 2000–02, an end-1998 top-10 US tech list needs delisted returns (Lucent, Nortel and others) from CRSP. On free survivor-only data that run is diagnostic, not a pass |
| Primary metric and pass | Maximum drawdown: P(challenger's drawdown ≥ benchmark's) < 0.05 on both universes, with the Sharpe point estimate no more than 0.05 lower. Report 2022 and Nov 2026 separately |
| Expected effect | Shallower drawdowns in semis-led selloffs, at a cost of −0.3 to −1.0 pp a year in AI-led bull years |
| Most likely failure | The cap rarely binds under inverse-vol, and when it does it cuts the names that drive terminal wealth (Bessembinder skew: about 4% of stocks created all net wealth, [ASU](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/department-finance/faculty-research/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)) |

### H7–H8 and H10, H13, H14: the remaining model-generated ideas (ranks 7, 8, 10, 13, 14)

These come from Grok and Astra. Each has a specification in the notes ([grok_astra_theories.md](../research_notes/Market%20theories%20retail%20edge/grok_astra_theories.md)), and each needs the fixes listed here before it is run. **Diversifier sleeves** (Grok's permanent 15% XLE rebalanced at 10/20% bands; Astra's 20% of equity dollars in XLP/XLV/XLU) win only on Sharpe in windows with an inflation shock. Defensive sectors are a low-beta tilt that a CAGR gate penalizes. The repo's closest analog, bonds and gold, missed its multiple-testing bar ([universe_results.md](universe_results.md)). Run each against a volatility-matched control so that "less tech beta" cannot pose as diversification. **Credit-panic commitment** (Astra #4: move 10% of NAV from bills into the basket for 252 sessions when high-yield OAS first crosses 7%) cannot be backtested from FRED as specified, because FRED's public ICE BofA HY OAS series now carries only about three years of history ([FRED BAMLH0A0HYM2](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/BAMLH0A0HYM2)). Use Moody's Baa minus 10-year (FRED `BAA10Y`) as the long proxy, set the threshold at its expanding 95th percentile, and apply H5's power rule. Grok's FLOT version uses the wrong instrument, and Astra's SOXX nest only adds semiconductor beta to an already semis-heavy book. **Patient sleeves** (Astra #6; Grok's "never sell a winner") are momentum on a hindsight-chosen list. Astra's control has to apply the same crash brake to both sleeves, otherwise a win just means more equity in the recovery. Grok's version removes the vol target, cap and trend filter together, which Grok itself expects to fail the Sharpe gate. **Bill income buying equities** (Astra #7) is mental accounting with an expected effect below its own hurdle. **Cash-secured puts held for three years after assignment** (Grok #8) inherit the repo's failed PutWrite result (P = 0.72; put-selling behaved as lower-beta equity, −35% in 2008) ([MATH.md](../MATH.md)). Its pricing uses an S&P vol index for a QQQ option, and below about $50,000 the account cannot meet the contract size anyway.

### H9. 52-week-high proximity (rank 9, ~5%)

The anchoring evidence is credible across the cross-section of all stocks ([George & Hwang](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/tgeorge/papers/gh5-paper.pdf)). Its spread depends on sentiment and has crash exposure ([Massey review](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/bitstreams/99e67c25-4ed2-4314-bd0b-062930e5742b/download)), and no post-2015 large-cap test was found. Inside a dozen correlated names the breadth is too small. A true 5 bp-a-day edge would need about 14 years of data to reach t = 2, and the repo's trend-signal rank IC reached only t = 1.66 ([MATH.md](../MATH.md)). Test: on the first trading day of each month, compute PTH = close / 252-session maximum close and rank the eligible names. Scale each inverse-vol base weight by a multiplier running linearly from 0.5 (lowest PTH) to 1.5 (highest), then re-apply caps and the vol target unchanged. Primary metric is Sharpe at P < 0.05 on both universes. The likeliest failure is no detectable difference, since this is momentum by another name in a book that already holds momentum through its 200-day rule.

### H11. Lazy Prices in megacaps (rank 11, ~4%)

The original effect is large and mechanistically appealing. Text changes predict earnings, news and bankruptcies, and attention closes the gap: when investors download both the current and prior 10-K, the drift shrinks ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/nov18/w25084.shtml)). For megacaps that cuts against the trade. Their filings are read by NLP vendors on the day they appear, and the paper is from 2020, so a post-publication haircut of about 58% is the right prior. The test runs in two stages so that a dead effect costs only one trial. Stage 1 checks the effect still exists. On the repo's point-in-time S&P 500 dataset (2016 onward), compute the cosine similarity between each 10-K's Item 1A (full document if 1A cannot be parsed) and the prior year's. Form value-weighted quintiles one trading day after filing, hold 12 months long non-changers and short changers, and require a Newey-West t > 3. Stage 2 runs only if Stage 1 passes. Any universe name in the bottom quintile of that year's S&P 500 similarity distribution gets its base weight halved for 252 sessions, with everything else frozen, and the primary metric is Sharpe at P < 0.05 on both universes. With about 12 filings a year, Stage 2 has almost no power, which is why its probability is so low.

### H12. The 4-week net-liquidity rule, pre-registered to fail (rank 12, ~3%)

This one is written down so that a failure is on record. In this research's own computation, holding the S&P only when the 4-week change in net liquidity (WALCL − TGA − RRP, lagged one week for the H.4.1 release) was positive improved Sharpe from 0.54 to 0.70 over 2003–2026, with a t-stat of about 2.2. But 13- and 26-week horizons both failed, the level correlation reversed sign in two regimes, and short-horizon swings are dominated by Treasury cash management rather than policy. A 40-week trend rule matched it on drawdowns. No monetary variable survives the Goyal-Welch-Zafirov re-test ([slides](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)), and Deutsche Bank calls the correlation "largely spurious" ([BNN Bloomberg](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/stock-fears-tied-to-us-debt-wave-miss-the-mark-deutsche-bank-says-1.1929652)). With RRP near zero, the series is now essentially the Fed balance sheet minus the Treasury's cash account. Test: compute NL from the Wednesday values of [WALCL](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WALCL), [WTREGEN](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WTREGEN) and [RRPONTSYD](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/RRPONTSYD), usable from the Friday close after the Thursday release. When NL's 4-week change is ≤ 0, multiply the frozen equity exposure by 0.5, stacking with the 200-day halving. The primary metric is Sharpe at P < 0.05 on both universes, and a pass also requires the 13-week version to show the same sign. The expected outcome is a fail driven by overlap with the 200-day filter and by the debt-ceiling and tax-date noise in the TGA.

## Conclusion

The debate among market theories does not end with a winner. It ends with a map of where inefficiency is allowed to survive: where arbitrage is costly, slow, tax-blind or career-threatening. Megacap tech is the one place on that map where none of those frictions bind on the arbitrageur. That is why fourteen hypotheses, two language models and this repo's 115-plus failed signals all point to the same conclusion. The frozen benchmark cannot be beaten by knowing more. It can be beaten, slightly, by being a different kind of investor than the one it was built to imitate. That investor is taxable and pays attention to lot ages, has no redemptions to fear, and has no committee that must approve re-risking after a panic. The edges are measured in tenths of a percent a year, and only H1 has a better than one-in-three chance of surviving. Expect the honest outcome of this slate to be one modest execution change and a list of well-documented failures.

Two implications follow. First, the research order should follow the probabilities rather than the excitement. The tax ledger needs no new data and no forecast. It is the only test whose mechanism cannot be front-run, and building it also gives H2, H4 and every later challenger a correct after-tax scoreboard. Second, the crisis-dependent ideas (H5, H6, the credit triggers) cannot be validated in any five-year window. They should be pre-registered now and judged on episode counts, so the next panic becomes a test rather than a temptation. The late-2026 setup, with record margin debt, record concentration and sharp AI-led selloffs, is exactly when an operator is most likely to override the model on a narrative. That makes writing these rules down before the next drawdown worth more than any of the hypotheses themselves.
