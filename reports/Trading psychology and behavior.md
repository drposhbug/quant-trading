# Your own biases cost more than the crowd's

As of late 2026, behavioral finance is far more useful for showing a systematic trader what to *avoid doing* than for supplying signals a daily, long-only US mega-cap tech system can harvest. The bias literature replicates strongly. Overconfident heavy traders lag the market by roughly 6–7 pp a year, investors realize gains about 1.5x as readily as losses, about 97% of persistent day traders lose money in two different countries, and in the 2020s retail losses moved into 0DTE and earnings options, where most of the loss is spread paid to market makers. Most of the *price patterns* those biases create live where this system cannot reach them: small, low-institutional-ownership, lottery-like stocks, mostly in the short leg, and published effects lose about 58–72% after publication. The two candidates with real large-cap support are residual short-term reversal and the overnight/intraday attention split. Both have already failed this repo's own tests on the 12-name universe, and a 12-name daily spread needs about 14 years of data to detect even a 5 bp/day effect. The flow-analyst picture also overturns the retail stereotype: from 2023 through mid-2026 retail was the market's most aggressive *dip buyer* in NVDA and TSLA, not its panic seller. The surviving behavioral "edge" for this system is therefore the oldest one. The equity premium partly pays people to endure the losses that myopic, loss-averse investors refuse to sit through. The system collects it only if its operator does not become that investor. The behavior gap is about 1.2 pp a year on average and over 2 pp for volatile funds, and it is a larger, better-documented threat to this system's realized return than any signal it could add.

## Biases cost retail traders 1 to 7 points a year, and the bill moved to options

The behavioral economists' core findings come from large brokerage datasets and have held for three decades. In Barber and Odean's 66,465-household sample (1991–96), the average household turned over about 80% of its portfolio a year. The most active fifth earned **10.0% net (geometric)** against 17.1% for the value-weighted market ([Odean, Berkeley](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/returns/Returns.html)). The paper's abstract frames the same gap as **11.4% versus 17.9%** on a different averaging convention, and either way it is roughly 6–7 pp a year. Overconfidence is the explanation the authors favor: men traded more and lost **0.94 pp a year more** to trading than women ([Barber & Odean, "Boys Will Be Boys"](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/gender/BoysWillBeBoys.pdf)). Odean's disposition-effect study found investors realized **14.8% of available gains but only 9.8% of available losses**, a ratio of about 1.5 ([NUS RMI, citing Odean Table I](https://rmi.nus.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/RMI-WPS-2019-12.pdf)). Attention biases buying more than selling. Buyers choose among thousands of stocks and sellers only among the ones they own, so retail is a net buyer of news-, volume- and big-move stocks, which then underperform what it sells ([Barber & Odean, RFS 2008](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/allthatglitters_rfs_2008.pdf)). Lottery preference costs holders of cheap, high-skew stocks **2–3 pp a year**, and the cost is largest for low-income investors ([Kumar, JF 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:64:y:2009:i:4:p:1889-1933)). Loss aversion itself survives meta-analysis of 607 estimates at a coefficient of **1.96**, a bit below Kahneman and Tversky's 2.25 and stable across study designs ([Brown et al., JEL 2024](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20221698)).

The skeptic's corrections are about mechanism, not existence. The disposition effect's probability of selling is V-shaped in gains and losses, which Ben-David and Hirshleifer read as belief-driven speculation rather than a pure preference for realizing gains ([RFS 2012](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v25y2012i8p2485-2532.html)). Rational models with adjustment costs can reproduce part of the trading-returns pattern, so how "hazardous" trading is remains disputed ([Bonaparte & Cooper, NBER w16022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16022/revisions/w16022.rev0.pdf)). None of this rescues the active trader's results, and the day-trading evidence is the most robust in the field. Across the entire Taiwanese market, **fewer than 1%** of day traders reliably earn positive net abnormal returns ([Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, JFM 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:1-24)). **97%** are likely to lose going forward, and **74%** of day-trading volume comes from traders with a history of losses, because losers quit too slowly ([Barber et al., RAPS 2020](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rasset:v:10:y:2020:i:1:p:61-93.)). In Brazilian index futures, about 97% of individuals who day-traded more than 300 days lost money, and only 1.1% earned more than the minimum wage ([Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101)). Two markets, two instruments and two eras give the same answer. Persisting despite losses is the behavioral signature: overconfidence plus biased self-attribution, or simply gambling utility.

The 2020s changed where retail loses, not whether it does. Robinhood herding into the day's most-bought stocks preceded **−3% five-day abnormal returns**, and **−6%** in the most extreme episodes. The herding was traced to the app's "Top Movers" display, and the losses were concentrated below $1B market cap ([Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz, JF 2022](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/SSRN-id3715077.pdf)). In S&P 500 options, about 75% of retail trades are 0DTE. Retail lost about **$358k a day** after daily expiries began in May 2022, and **about 60%** of that was transaction costs and spreads ([Bloomberg on Beckmeyer, Branger & Gayda](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-04-21/day-traders-lose-358-000-per-day-gambling-on-zero-day-options)). Retail option buyers ahead of earnings lose **5–9%** on average and **10–14%** on high-expected-volatility announcements, about $3B over a decade. They overpay for inflated implied volatility and then fail to close after the event, which is a disposition effect in options form ([MIT Sloan on de Silva, Smith & So](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/retail-investors-lose-big-options-markets-research-shows)). The common thread is paying for skewness and immediacy. A long-only stock system that trades slowly simply does not pay that bill.

Professionals are not immune; their biases are just more selective. Across 783 institutional portfolios and about 4.4M trades (2000–2016), managers' buys added value, but their **sells underperformed a random-selling counterfactual by about 80 bp a year**. The same managers sold well around earnings announcements, when information was salient, so the skill exists but is not applied to exits ([Akepanidtaworn, Di Mascio, Imas & Schmidt, NBER w29076](https://nber.org/papers/w29076)). Professional buying is also the least driven by attention ([Barber & Odean 2008](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/allthatglitters_rfs_2008.pdf)). For a systematic trader the transferable lesson is concrete: discretionary exits are where skilled people leak money, so exits are the first thing to mechanize.

## Retail turned into the market's most reliable dip buyer

The stereotype that retail panics, capitulates and sells the bottom does not describe 2020–2026, and a strategy built on it is trading a ghost. On 3 April 2025, during the tariff selloff, individuals net bought **$4.7bn** of US equities, the largest daily inflow in about a decade. JPMorgan contrasted this explicitly with March 2020, when individuals sold on down days ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/15/stocks-tariffs-tesla-nvidia-palantir)). Retail flows then set back-to-back monthly records of **$36bn in March and $40bn in April 2025**, and retail's share of order flow hit **36%** on 29 April ([JPMorgan AM](https://am.jpmorgan.com/us/en/asset-management/liq/insights/market-insights/market-updates/on-the-minds-of-investors/who-is-buying-us-equities)). Investors kept buying leveraged TSLA and NVDA funds while those stocks fell more than 30% ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/etfs/leveraged-etfs-ride-retail-trading-boom-as-single-stock-bets-redefine-market-stress-playbook/265389)). In 2026 the pattern intensified. Citadel Securities measured retail dollar demand in early 2026 at about **25% above the 2021 peak** ([Citadel Securities](https://www.citadelsecurities.com/news-and-insights/in_the_news/citadel-securities-sees-retail-crowd-buying-dips-at-record-pace/)), and reported retail buying about **3.5x its daily average on SPX down days** in 1H 2026, "the strongest buy-the-dip behavior in our dataset" ([Citadel Securities 1H 2026](https://www.citadelsecurities.com/?p=43645)). Even the academic baseline argues against panic: Robinhood users *added* to holdings in the March 2020 crash, and the aggregate crowd portfolio showed good timing and positive alpha from 2018 to 2020 ([Welch, JF 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27866)).

This flow lands squarely on the system's universe. NVDA, TSLA and PLTR led H1 2025 retail volume. Retail traded about $62bn a day, and over half of consumer-discretionary net buying was TSLA ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/retail-trading-remains-elevated-favorite-stock-shifts); [TipRanks](https://www.tipranks.com/news/nvda-tsla-pltr-retail-investors-traded-a-record-6-6-trillion-of-stocks-in-years-first-half)). On one venue, TSLA alone was **11.6%** of retail volume and NVDA 6.8% ([MEMX](https://memx.com/insights/retail-trading-insights)). Individuals make up about **90%** of leveraged-ETF trading while being about 5.5% of overall equity trading, and the biggest single-stock products track TSLA, META and NVDA ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/etfs/leveraged-etfs-ride-retail-trading-boom-as-single-stock-bets-redefine-market-stress-playbook/265389)). Those products rebalance in the direction of the day's move near the close ([Bank Underground](https://bankunderground.co.uk/2023/08/31/leveraged-and-inverse-etfs-the-exotic-side-of-exchange-traded-funds/amp/)), and they lag their simple leveraged benchmark by 0.28% (3x) to 0.49% (−2x) from rebalancing alone ([Alpha Architect on Bessembinder 2025](https://alphaarchitect.com/leveraged-single-stock-etfs/)).

The flow analyst's reading is that retail demand in mega-caps acts as **price support and a liquidity source, not an information signal**. Buying that arrives *on* down days probably makes selloffs in NVDA and TSLA shallower than they would otherwise be. The risk is conditional. The crowd was trained by V-shaped recoveries in 2020, 2022–23 and 2025, it is concentrated and partly leveraged, and a 2000–02-style regime where dips keep falling would hit it all at once. The first signs of fatigue have appeared. JPMorgan flagged weekly retail purchases slowing about 30% after a near-record February 2026 ([Sherwood News](https://www.sherwood.news/markets/jpmorgan-warns-on-the-first-persistent-signs-of-weakness-from-retail-traders/)), and other reports describe rotation out of NVDA into SK Hynix and Marvell ([Sherwood](https://www.sherwood.news/markets/retail-traders-are-bailing-on-nvidia-selling-the-most-in-a-decade)). If retail flow is useful at all, the useful part is a *change* in its level, which flags that a support layer is thinning. That makes it a risk input, and the data are proprietary press-released aggregates (JPM, Vanda, Citadel) that cannot be backtested on free data.

Two popular crowd narratives fail under scrutiny. The "0DTE gamma crash" story is not supported: 0DTE is now about **59% of SPX option volume** (2025), with retail about half of it ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/spx-0-dte-options-jump-to-61-share-on-retail-resurgence)). Yet aggregate 0DTE gamma is *inversely* related to intraday volatility, and the effects do not carry overnight or into the next day ([Dim, Eraker & Vilkov via Harbourfront](https://harbourfrontquant.substack.com/p/the-rise-of-0dte-options-cause-for-concern-or-business-as-usual)). The predictable hedging and LETF effects sit at the close and are short-lived ([Barbon et al., SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3925725)). For a once-a-day system, 0DTE flow is noise. The second narrative, "fade the retail crowd," holds only for *herding spikes in small attention stocks*. Retail order-imbalance measures that once looked informative ([Boehmer, Jones, Zhang & Zhang, JF 2021](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:76:y:2021:i:5:p:2249-2305)) turn out to capture less than a third of retail trades and misclassify some ([TCU / Barber et al.](https://www.neeley.tcu.edu/centers/lkcm-center-for-financial-studies/finance-conference/identifying-market-maker-trades)). Aggregate, slow-moving retail portfolios can do fine while attention spikes reverse, and both findings can be true at once.

Sentiment extremes are the weakest of the popular tools. Survey and composite indicators show unstable short-horizon power. Even at extreme bullish readings the market rose about half the time ([Option Alpha summarizing SF Fed work](https://optionalpha.com/podcast/predictive-power-of-investor-sentiment)), and AAII's own study finds that only extreme *pessimism* has a contrarian record ([AAII](https://www.aaii.com/sentimentsurvey?a=blog20190321)). The version that survives out of sample is a constructed, PLS-aligned Baker-Wurgler index at monthly-plus horizons, in which high sentiment predicts low returns ([Huang, Jiang, Tu & Zhou, RFS 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v28y2015i3p791-837..html)). The cross-sectional sentiment effect sits in the *short leg* and has no relation to long-leg returns ([Stambaugh, Yu & Yuan, NBER w16898](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16898.pdf)), so a long-only book cannot use it. Capitulation signals look better than they are. S&P reports **88%** positive 12-month returns after 557 days with VIX above 30 ([Indexology](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2022/03/15/markets-remained-volatile-but-no-need-to-panic/)), but counting *independent crossings* leaves 24 episodes since 1990, of which about 75% were positive at 12 months. 2008 returned −10.8%, and the median turns positive only from about six months out ([eco3min](https://eco3min.fr/en/vix-contrarian-buy-signal-historical-thresholds-equity-rebound/)). The drop from 88% to 75% is a clean demonstration of how overlapping daily observations manufacture confidence. The repo found the same thing at short horizons: VIX level, term structure and 5-day change all failed at 1 and 5 days among **0 of 44** macro tests ([macro_results.md](macro_results.md)).

## Behavioral footprints mostly vanish inside twelve mega-caps

The skeptic's prior is set by decay. Across 97 predictors, returns fall **58% after publication**, and the fall is larger for liquid, low-idiosyncratic-risk stocks ([McLean & Pontiff, JF 2016](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf)). Using post-2005 data the decay is about **72%** ([Fed FEDS 2021-037](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2021-037pap.pdf)). With NYSE breakpoints and value weights, **64% of 447 anomalies** are insignificant, because microcaps are about 3% of market cap but about 60% of names ([Hou, Xue & Zhang, NBER w23394](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23394.pdf)). Behavioral anomalies are the hardest hit, because their fuel is retail attention, limits to arbitrage and short-sale constraints. All twelve names here are the opposite: institutionally owned, covered by dozens of analysts and cheap to arbitrage. The power arithmetic explains why even the plausible rows cannot be rescued by more careful testing. Suppose the daily top-half-minus-bottom-half spread among 12 correlated names has a standard deviation around 1.5%. Then reaching t = 2 on a true **5 bp/day** edge needs about 3,600 trading days, roughly 14 years, and a **2 bp/day** edge needs about 90 years. Apply a 58–72% decay haircut to a large-cap reversal premium and you land in the 1–4 bp/day range. The repo's 2015–2026 sample can detect only effects larger than anything the literature supports, so a positive backtest would be weak evidence and a null says little. Costs make it worse. A daily reversal tilt can turn over 20–50% of the book, which at 2–5 bp round trip eats the same 0.5–2.5 bp/day the signal might earn. This is the same fundamental-law wall that removed the trend and reversal signals, where an IC of 0.006 on eight names gave an IR near 0.27 before costs ([MATH.md](../MATH.md)).

There is one behavioral effect this system already collects, and it is the one with the deepest theory behind it. Benartzi and Thaler showed that loss-averse investors who evaluate their portfolios frequently demand a large equity premium, and that annual evaluation fits the observed premium ([QJE 1995](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:110:y:1995:i:1:p:73-92.)). Experiments confirm that more frequent feedback produces less risk-taking ([Gneezy & Potters 1997](https://Rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/evaluation-periods.pdf)). The premium is partly a payment for tolerating losses that myopic investors will not tolerate. A vol-targeted, long-only book that stays invested through drawdowns collects it. Interpreted behaviorally, the shipped risk engine is a device that keeps the drawdowns small enough for the operator to stay put: same Sharpe as equal weight, −26.6% max drawdown instead of −44.3% ([backtest_results.md](backtest_results.md)). The steelman for an additional tilt is the retail dip-buying support layer, which may make mega-cap selloffs shallower and recoveries faster than in history. It argues against adding fear-driven de-risking rules. It does not justify a new buy signal, because no free, backtestable series captures it.

## The trader's own biases are the system's largest remaining risk

Once prediction is off the table, the biggest behavioral variable left is the person running the system. The average dollar in US funds earned about **8.7% a year versus 9.9%** for the funds themselves over the ten years to end-2025, a behavior gap of about 1.2 pp, or about 12% of the return ([Morningstar Mind the Gap 2025](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025)). The gap scales with exactly the features this system has. It is about **0.4 pp for the least volatile funds and 2.1 pp for the most volatile** ([Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2026/08/21/what-advisors-can-learn-from-the-investor-return-gap)). It is widest in sector funds ([WealthManagement](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/investing-strategies/investors-miss-out-on-12-of-fund-returns-over-decade)), and it reached **3.6%** in the highest tracking-error decile ([Research Affiliates via Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2018/10/24/the-biggest-failure-in-investment-management-how-smart-beta-can-make-it-better-or-worse)). This is a concentrated, 20%-volatility tech sector book whose operator will instinctively compare it with QQQ and SPY. Allocation and target-date funds, where nobody makes discretionary decisions, have the narrowest gaps ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025)). The lesson is fewer decisions, not more willpower. DALBAR's headline gaps swing from 8.5 pp to 0.7 pp in consecutive years ([DALBAR 2026](https://www.dalbar.com/PressReleases/doc/2026QAIBReportPressRelease.pdf)), so its single-year figures are noise. Morningstar's dollar-weighted method is the estimate to trust.

The decision-science evidence says what to automate and what discretion to keep. Mechanical prediction beats or ties clinical judgment, about **10% more accurate on average**, regardless of task or the judge's experience ([Grove, Zald et al. 2000](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/clinical-versus-mechanical-prediction-a-meta-analysis/)). After 9,000+ hedge funds were adjusted for risk, investors' lower confidence in systematic funds "is not justified" ([Harvey et al. 2017](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P130_Man_vs_machine.pdf)). Yet people abandon an algorithm after watching it err, even when it beat the human ([Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey 2015](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/20-Dietvorst-Simmons-Massey-2015.pdf)). A system's first bad month is exactly when its operator is most likely to override it. The tested fix is to let people adjust the output slightly. Adherence rises sharply, results improve, and the effect is "relatively insensitive" to how big the allowed adjustment is ([Dietvorst et al., Management Science 2018](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Dietvorst-Overcoming-Algorithm-Aversion.pdf)). A small, logged knob therefore beats a full veto. Human input earns its place when it enters as a structured input rather than a veto. Feeding analyst forecasts into an AI model avoided about 90% of the humans' extreme errors ([Cao et al., JFE 2024](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3840538)). Quant models also lag after definitional changes in their data ([Bocconi WP](https://accounting.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/files/media/attachments/DGY_20210220210222160122.pdf)). That is a legitimate case for human review of *data*, not of positions.

The practitioner frameworks split cleanly into tested and untested. Outside-view and base-rate training improved forecast accuracy **6–11%** in under an hour ([Chang et al., JDM 2016](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/16/16511/jdm16511.html)). Less frequent evaluation reduces loss-averse behavior (Gneezy-Potters above). Overfitting probability rises with every configuration tried ([Bailey et al., SSRN 2326253](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)). Pre-mortems have thin support ([Jason Collins review](https://corporate.jasoncollins.blog/premortem)). Checklists work only when actually followed: the WHO surgical checklist roughly halved mortality in pilots but showed no effect across Ontario ([MDedge](https://mdedge.com/familymedicine/article/80926/health-policy/surgical-checklists-failed-improve-outcomes)). Trading-coach programs, journals, Douglas's "Trading in the Zone" and Dalio's Principles have no outcome tests. The one rigorous trader-emotion study found that traders with stronger emotional reactions to gains *and* losses did worse, but it tested no remedy ([Lo, Repin & Steenbarger 2005](https://web.mit.edu/Alo/www/Papers/online.html)). Operational failure deserves its own control. Knight Capital lost about **$440M in 45 minutes** when old code ran on 8 of 9 servers and could not be stopped quickly ([Chicago Fed](https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-letter/2012/october-303)).

Applied to this repo, much of the protection already exists. Holm-corrected, pre-registered tests, DSR and PBO, a look-ahead test in `test_model.py`, a ±1 pp no-trade band and close-of-day automation already neutralize overconfidence in signals, data-snooping and overtrading. The remaining exposures are human. Two show up in the repo's own history. The universe itself carries **hindsight bias**: the names were chosen knowing they became the decade's AI winners, so absolute returns are inflated and only same-name comparisons are fair ([MATH.md](../MATH.md)). Second, the vol target was raised from 15% to 25% on 2026-11-18 "to target return." That change is defensible on Kelly arithmetic, but it was made by a human, after a strong run, for return reasons, and it moved the drawdown result versus QQQ from pass (p = 0.019) to fail (p = 0.15). That is the template of a recency-driven change, and the controls below exist to catch it. The table maps each control to the bias it blocks and its evidence grade.

| Control | Bias it blocks | Evidence grade | Status in repo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fully automated entries *and* exits | Discretionary selling worse than random; disposition effect | Empirical (Akepanidtaworn; Grove-Meehl) | Done |
| Pre-registration, trial counting, DSR/PBO, Holm | Overconfidence in backtests, data-snooping | Empirical (Bailey et al.; Harvey-Liu-Zhu) | Done; true trial count exceeds the 60-variant grid |
| ±1 pp no-trade band, daily close-only trading | Overtrading | Empirical (Barber-Odean) | Done |
| Change control: parameter changes only on a fixed calendar, never in a drawdown or right after a strong run, re-run with updated trial count | Recency, return-chasing | Inferred from behavior-gap drivers | **Add**; apply retroactively to the 25% vol target |
| Drawdown tiers set in advance from the bootstrap drawdown distribution: review at the 95th-percentile drawdown, halve size at about 1.5x, halt to paper at about 2x | Panic liquidation; also the sunk-cost refusal to stop | Practitioner convention; multipliers untested | **Add**; written, not felt |
| Bounded override (e.g. ±10–20% of target gross, logged with a reason and graded against the model counterfactual) | Algorithm aversion | Empirical (Dietvorst 2018); Man Group anecdotes ([Man Institute](https://www.man.com/maninstitute/commiting-to-behavioural-recommendations)) | **Add** |
| Ops kill switch separate from P&L (stale data, order or notional limits, broker errors) | Runaway code, panic during outages | Case evidence (Knight) | Partial (1% trade floor, stale-order cancel) |
| Weekly or monthly P&L review; alerts only for ops faults and drawdown tiers | Myopic loss aversion | Empirical (Benartzi-Thaler; Gneezy-Potters) | **Add** |
| Track dollar-weighted vs time-weighted return of the live account | The personal behavior gap | Empirical (Morningstar method) | **Add**; one line in the monitor |
| No leveraged single-stock ETFs, 0DTE or pre-earnings options "to express a view" | Lottery preference, overpaying for skew | Empirical (Kumar; Beckmeyer; de Silva) | Keep out |
| 24–48 h cooling-off before any discretionary action after a large gain or loss | Emotional reactivity | Folklore, consistent with Lo-Repin-Steenbarger | Optional |

## Conclusion

The research reverses the usual reason for studying trader psychology. Behavioral biases are real, large and stable, from the 1990s brokerage records through 0DTE in 2025. But the prices they distort are the ones this system does not hold, on the side of the book it cannot take, at horizons it does not trade. In mega-cap tech the crowd's bias in 2025–26 was not panic but a conditioned, leveraged dip-buying reflex. That reflex is a support layer while recoveries keep coming and a correlated risk if one does not. It belongs in the risk discussion, not in the signal set. The search for a behavioral alpha in twelve names is closed for now. Residual reversal and the overnight split, the two credible candidates, already failed here, and the remaining ones cannot be detected at the effect sizes the literature allows.

What remains is a role reversal. This system is designed to be the patient counterparty to myopic loss aversion. It holds through drawdowns, sells only by rule and trades rarely, which collects the premium that impatient money pays. Its realistic failure mode is its operator turning into that impatient money. That means overriding after the first bad quarter, raising risk after a hot year, or abandoning the model at the drawdown trough, which is the exact pattern that costs volatile-fund investors about 2 pp a year. The highest-value next steps are cheap and need no new data. Write down the change-control and drawdown-tier rules before the next drawdown, log every override against its counterfactual, and measure the live account's dollar-weighted return against the model's time-weighted one. That last number will show, better than any backtest, whether the edge survived contact with its operator.
