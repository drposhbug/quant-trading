## Thread 1 — Fat tails, Mandelbrot / power laws, long memory in volatility, and Taleb on sizing

### Takeaway
Fat tails are the most robust fact in this whole literature: return tails decay roughly as a power law with exponent near 3 (the "inverse cubic law"), so variance exists but higher moments become unreliable. Volatility, unlike returns, shows long memory (absolute-return autocorrelation persisting for thousands of days). Returns themselves show little long memory once short-range dependence is controlled for. For sizing, the practical lesson is that risk models calibrated on Gaussian variance understate single-event ruin risk. It is not a lesson that tails are forecastable.

### Cited Findings
**Round 1. Q: Are return tails really power laws, and with what exponent?**
- Gopikrishnan, Meyer, Amaral & Stanley (1998) used every trade on NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ for 1994–95, about 40 million data points. The cumulative distribution has a power-law tail with α ≈ 3: α = 3.1 ± 0.03 on the positive tail and 2.84 ± 0.12 on the negative tail, fitted from roughly 2 to 100 standard deviations — [Gopikrishnan et al. 1998, arXiv cond-mat/9803374](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9803374); [EPJ B PDF](https://amaral.northwestern.edu/media/publication_pdfs/Gopikrishnan-1998-Eur.Phys.J.B-3-139.pdf)
- That α ≈ 3 sits "well outside the Lévy stable regime (0 < α < 2)". This contradicts the original Mandelbrot stable-Paretian hypothesis of infinite variance, while still rejecting the Gaussian. The distribution is not stable, and moments above 3 diverge — [Gopikrishnan et al. 1998](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/9803374)
- Replication on Indian index data found α ≈ 3 at both 1-minute and 1-day scales. The authors read this as evidence of "universality" — [Pan & Sinha 2006, arXiv physics/0607014](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0607014)

**Round 2. Follow-up: Mandelbrot also claimed "long memory" (Hurst H > 0.5). Is that in returns or in volatility?**
- Lo (1991) proposed a modified R/S statistic that is robust to short-range dependence. He found no evidence of long-range dependence in daily or monthly CRSP index returns, and argued that earlier classical R/S findings (e.g. Greene & Fielitz 1977) reflected short-term correlation — [Lobato & Savin, "Real and Spurious Long Memory Properties of Stock Market Data"](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/em/papers/9605/9605004.pdf)
- Ding, Granger & Engle (1993) found strong long memory in transformations of absolute S&P 500 daily returns (1928–1992). Daily absolute returns show significant positive autocorrelation out to lags of about 2,700 days — [Lobato & Savin](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/em/papers/9605/9605004.html)
- Caveats. Non-stationarity (structural breaks) can produce apparent long memory and is "a plausible explanation" of the Ding et al. result — [Lobato & Savin](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/em/papers/9605/9605004.pdf). Lo's modified R/S is biased toward rejecting long-range dependence (Willinger et al. 1999) — [summary via search results, see Lobato & Savin and related](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/em/papers/9605/9605004.pdf). Regime-switching multifractal models can mimic long-range dependence over finite samples ("true vs apparent scaling") — [Calvet/Fisher-style MSM proximity paper, arXiv 0704.1338](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/0704.1338)

**Round 3. Follow-up: What does Taleb say this implies for position sizing?**
- Taleb's *Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails* (Technical Incerto) is about the misapplication of conventional statistical techniques to fat-tailed distributions. Its chapters include "Ruin and Path Dependence" and "What To Do?" — [Taleb, arXiv 2001.10488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10488)
- Under subexponential (fat-tailed) distributions, ruin is more likely to come from a single extreme event than from an accumulation of moderate losses, a framing attributed to Taleb — [commentary on Taleb, dyrehaugen notes](https://dyrehaugen.github.io/rsts/fat-tails.html)
- The ergodicity argument (time average differs from ensemble average) has a standard fix: maximize expected log wealth, which is equivalent to the Kelly (1956) criterion. A critic notes that Peters/Taleb-style ergodicity claims beyond this are often overstated — [Advisor Perspectives, "Understanding Fat Tail Returns"](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2021/03/01/understanding-fat-tail-returns)

### Inferences
- With α ≈ 3, variance is finite but its sample estimate converges slowly, and kurtosis is unreliable or infinite. Vol-targeting (e.g. a 20% vol target) is still well-defined, but realized drawdowns will exceed Gaussian-implied ones. Stress-size on a tail scenario (e.g. a 5–10 sigma daily move), not on sigma alone.
- Long memory sits in volatility, not direction. Volatility clustering is exploitable for risk scaling (forecast vol, then scale exposure), but this does not give a directional return edge.
- Kelly on fat tails: estimation error in the mean, combined with tail risk, argues for fractional Kelly. This is our inference from the ruin framing, not a number taken from Taleb's text.

### Gaps
- Could not access Mandelbrot (1963, cotton prices) directly for his original α estimate. It is commonly quoted as about 1.7, but it is unverified here.
- Did not read Taleb's chapters 3.11–3.12 or his kappa metric numbers; his specific sizing prescription is not quoted.
- No sourced Hurst-exponent values (e.g. H for realized vol) were retrieved. Literature values of H ≈ 0.1 for "rough volatility" (Gatheral et al.) were not checked in this session.

## Thread 2 — Sornette's LPPL/LPPLS bubble-crash prediction: actual forecast record; agent-based models (Santa Fe market)

### Takeaway
LPPL has a few celebrated real-time calls: oil in July 2008, Shanghai in August 2009 and Shanghai in June 2015. It also has a documented mixed record on its pre-registered "Financial Bubble Experiment": 2 of 4 first-round assets clearly changed regime inside the forecast window, 1 had turned before publication, and 1 did not burst. Independent critiques show that the 7-parameter fit is sloppy and sensitive to the fitting window. They also show that significance tests using conventional critical values over-reject, which yields false positives. No independent scorecard of FCO hit and false-alarm rates was found. The agent-based Santa Fe market shows that bubbles and crashes can emerge endogenously from adaptive learning, but it produces no trading signal.

### Cited Findings
**Round 1. Q: What is the claimed real-time record?**
- Sornette's group cites two real-time ex-ante predictions: the peak of the oil-price bubble in early July 2008, and the burst of the Shanghai bubble in early August 2009. They say out-of-sample performance, "quantified by error diagrams", shows "high significance". The method was also applied to "negative bubbles" (rebounds) — [Sornette et al., "Diagnosis and Prediction of Tipping Points in Financial Markets: Crashes and Rebounds", arXiv 1001.0265](https://arxiv.org/abs/1001.0265)
- Chinese 2015 bubble. The FCO published a real-time diagnostic of the Chinese bubble (which developed from mid-2014) on its website and in its June 2015 "FCO Cockpit" report. The paper documents those ex-ante outputs — [Sornette, Demos, Zhang, Cauwels, Filimonov & Zhang, "Real-Time Prediction and Post-Mortem Analysis of the Shanghai 2015 Stock Market Bubble and Crash", J. Investment Strategies 4:77–95; SFI RP 15-32](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:chf:rpseri:rp1532)
- Size of the 2015 crash: the SSEC fell more than 43% from its 12 June 2015 peak to the 26 August 2015 trough, and the SZSC fell 45% — [Shu & Zhu, arXiv 1905.09633](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.09633). A retrospective back-test (not ex ante) says t_c "can be well predicted… as far back as two months before" the crash — [same](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.09633)

**Round 2. Follow-up: Was there a pre-registered test, and how did it score?**
- The Financial Bubble Experiment (launched 2 November 2009) published diagnoses before outcomes. The first report flagged three bubbles: IBOVESPA, a Merrill Lynch corporate bond index and gold spot. Cotton futures was added on 23 December 2009 — [Sornette, Woodard et al., arXiv 0911.0454](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.0454); [Volume III, arXiv 1011.2882](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.2882)
- Self-reported outcome. IBOVESPA and gold "showed clear signs of changing from a bubble regime" within the forecast quantile windows. The ML bond index had already shifted before publication. Cotton "was and still is in a bubble". **Score: 2 clear regime changes within window out of 4, 1 early (arguably a miss on timing), 1 no-burst** — [arXiv 1011.2882 / 0911.0454](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1011.2882)
- Press assessment was "mixed". MIT Tech Review noted the ML index was already collapsing at prediction time, and cotton had not collapsed — [MIT Technology Review 2010](https://www.technologyreview.com/2010/06/02/203082/econophysicist-accurately-forecasts-gold-price-collapse/amp/). Phys.org described the gold outcome as "a partial success or a failure", since the FCO's own indicators said the bubble had not ended — [Phys.org 2010](https://phys.org/news/2010-05-financial.html). Basel economist Silvio Borner said Sornette "may have gone too far in the interpretation" — [swissinfo.ch](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/business/test-may-burst-myth-around-financial-bubbles/8818488)
- Gold forecasts were published as probability windows, e.g. a 20/80 quantile window for the critical time of about November 2009 to February 2010 — [arXiv 0911.0454](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0911.0454). Later volumes covered 7 bubbles (Vol II) and a larger set with date windows for 25 of 27 (Vol III), but no aggregate outcome tally was found — [Vol II, arXiv 1005.5675](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1005.5675)

**Round 3. Follow-up (sharper): What do critics say about false positives and fragility?**
- Brée & Joseph (IRFA 2013, Hang Seng). The posited mechanism (influence percolation plus a martingale condition) holds for only about half of previously reported pre-crash bubbles, and the data support the LPPL fit "only partially". They also note that the LPPL fit could have anticipated the Hang Seng fall in the global downturn — [Brée & Joseph, arXiv 1002.1010](https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.1010v2)
- Brée, Challet & Peirano: LPPL functions are "intrinsically very hard to fit" and "very sloppy in their parameters". The predicted crash time is sensitive to the event-window size, and fitting 7 parameters to noisy data invites overfitting — [arXiv 1107.3171](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1107.3171)
- Some log-periodic precursors are followed by a smooth draw-down or even a larger draw-up, not a crash — [Brée & Joseph / LPPL literature, arXiv 1002.1010](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1002.1010v2)
- Feigenbaum (2001) found the log-periodic component lost significance when the final pre-crash year was excluded. Sornette & Johansen replied that removing the data nearest the critical point is "naive" — [Sornette & Johansen, "Significance of log-periodic precursors", arXiv cond-mat/0106520](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0106520). Laloux, Potters, Cont, Aguilar & Bouchaud (EPL 1999) critically reviewed the claims of crash predictability from log-periodicity — [EPL 1999](https://doi.org/10.1209/epl/i1999-00122-9)
- False-positive rate dispute. Lin et al. (2014) reported a very low false-positive rate (0.2%) on synthetic series. A 2025 IRFA paper argues that conventional tabulated critical values give inflated rejection rates on S&P 500 monthly and daily data, that a bootstrap restores nominal size, and that the bootstrap "overturns many purported signatures" — [IRFA 2025, via Univ. of Vaasa repository (search abstract; full text unreachable)](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/bitstreams/e834bca8-9b22-45b6-906c-136d867ec80c/download)
- Supportive long-sample study. On monthly S&P 500 data from 1791 to 2014, the DS-LPPLS method reportedly identifies all well-documented bubbles, with "significantly smaller numbers of false negatives and false positives" than exponential-fit or GSADF methods. The abstract gives no counts — [Zhang, Sornette et al., Physica A 458 (2016)](https://repository.up.ac.za/handle/2263/53236). Demirer, Demos, Gupta & Sornette (Quant Finance 2019) report that multi-scale LPPLS confidence indicators captured Black Monday, dot-com and subprime "ex-ante" on S&P 500, FTSE and Nikkei — [econpapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:19:y:2019:i:5:p:843-858)

**Round 4. Follow-up: Do agent-based models (Santa Fe market) support "endogenous crashes"?**
- Arthur, Holland, LeBaron, Palmer & Tayler, "Asset Pricing Under Endogenous Expectations in an Artificial Stock Market" (SFI WP 96-12-093). Heterogeneous agents continually adapt their forecasting rules — [Arthur et al. PDF](https://sites.santafe.edu/~wbarthur/Papers/Artif_Mkt.pdf); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wop:safiwp:96-12-093)
- The key result is that a single parameter, the agents' learning speed (how often they revise forecasting rules), shifts the market between regimes. With slow learning the market converges to rational-expectations / efficient-market behaviour. With fast learning it enters a "complex" regime with technical trading, bubbles, crashes and GARCH-like volatility clustering — [Ehrentreich 2002 review](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/comp/papers/0209/0209001.pdf); [Tesfatsion SFI-ASM notes](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/SFIStockMarketModel.LTDetailed.htm)

### Inferences
- The only pre-registered batch found (4 assets) scores about 50% clean hits. That is far too small a sample to separate from chance, especially since "regime change within a quantile window" is a loose criterion for a trending asset.
- The famous successes (oil 2008, China 2009 and 2015) were large, fast, retail-driven bubbles. Selection of which calls get publicized is a real concern. No source gives the denominator: all FCO alerts issued versus crashes realized.
- For a retail trader, LPPLS is at best a "bubble-regime" filter (de-risk or avoid shorting puts). It is not a crash-timing tool. The t_c estimate is window-sensitive, and the bootstrap critique suggests many published "signatures" are false positives.
- The Santa Fe market gives theoretical cover for endogenous crashes, but no forecasting content.

### Gaps
- **No independent hit/false-alarm table for the FCO** (e.g. all "bubble warnings" since 2008 versus subsequent drawdowns) was found. The error-diagram numbers in arXiv 1001.0265 were not extractable from the abstract.
- Outcomes of FBE Volumes II–III (7 and 25+ bubbles) were not retrieved. FCO forecasts for bitcoin, 2018, 2020 and 2021 meme/tech were not checked.
- Exact LPPLS false-positive and bootstrap-overturn percentages from the 2025 IRFA paper are unknown (server unreachable).

## Thread 3 — Self-organized criticality and early-warning signals (critical slowing down, variance, absorption ratio): out-of-sample evidence

### Takeaway
If crashes were critical transitions, lag-1 autocorrelation should rise before them (critical slowing down). Empirically it does not. A 5-index, 14-crash study found no CSD. Variance did rise before every crash, but in the 115-year Dow sample about 7 of 16 strong variance signals were false alarms, and the signals give no timing. Diks–Hommes–Wang found CSD only before 1987. The Kritzman absorption ratio (rising correlation concentration) has strong in-sample support from its own authors ("most significant drawdowns preceded by spikes"), but no independent out-of-sample hit/false-alarm study was found. Net: early-warning signals are useful as fragility or risk-scaling inputs, not as crash predictors.

### Cited Findings
**Round 1. Q: Does critical slowing down (rising autocorrelation) precede crashes?**
- Guttal, Raghavendra, Goel & Hoarau (PLoS ONE 2016) tested 5 indices: Dow (1929, 1987, 2000, 2008), S&P 500 and NASDAQ (1987, 2000, 2008), and DAX and FTSE (2000, 2008), which is 14 crash-index pairs. Lag-1 autocorrelation showed no meaningful rise before any crash, with trends comparable to surrogate data. Conclusion: meltdowns are "not critical transitions", more like noise-induced (stochastic) transitions — [Guttal et al. 2016, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0144198)
- Diks, Hommes & Wang (Empirical Economics 2019) tested four historical crises. They found significant CSD before Black Monday 1987 only, with results "mixed or insignificant" for more recent crises — [Diks, Hommes & Wang 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00181-018-1527-3)
- Gatfaoui, Nagot & de Peretti (2016, 10 European exchanges plus the US): results were "mitigated" and depended on the series and the indicator. First-order autocorrelation on log indices produced false alarms — [HAL working paper](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-01339815v2/file/16045R.pdf)

**Round 2. Follow-up (quantify): If variance rises, what are the hit and false-alarm rates?**
- Guttal et al.: detrended variance (and low-frequency power spectrum) showed strong upward trends before **all** crashes studied (hit rate about 100% in-sample). But over 115 years of Dow data, 16 strong-trend events (Kendall τ > 0.9) were found. These comprised the 4 major crashes, 4 minor crises and **7 false alarms**, roughly a 44% false-alarm share of signals. The paper is internally inconsistent on whether 4 or 5 signals preceded the major crashes. Rising variance "does not indicate when" the crash occurs — [Guttal et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0144198)
- In 2011–2015 the Dow, S&P and FTSE showed no significant trend, while NASDAQ and DAX showed strong variance rises. The authors call these mixed signals; no crash followed in that window — [Guttal et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0144198)
- Plain return volatility (as opposed to detrended variance) did not trend consistently before some crashes, so it is not a reliable substitute — [same](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0144198)

**Round 3. Follow-up: Does the Kritzman absorption ratio (correlation concentration) do better, especially out of sample?**
- Definition: the AR is the fraction of total variance of a set of asset returns explained ("absorbed") by a fixed number of eigenvectors. Higher AR means a more tightly coupled, fragile market — [Kritzman, Li, Page & Rigobon, "Principal Components as a Measure of Systemic Risk", SSRN 1633027 (Scribd mirror)](https://www.scribd.com/document/62991148/SSRN-id1633027); [OFR survey of 31 systemic-risk measures, Bisias, Flood, Lo & Valavanis](https://financialresearch.gov/working-papers/files/OFRwp0001_BisiasFloodLoValavanis_ASurveyOfSystemicRiskAnalytics.pdf)
- Authors' claims: "most significant U.S. stock market drawdowns were preceded by spikes in the absorption ratio", and most global financial crises coincided with positive AR shifts. Changes in AR (not its level) carry most of the fragility information — [search abstract of SSRN 1633027 / Kinlaw et al. follow-up](https://www.scribd.com/document/488336818/systemic)
- A press summary of an MSCI USA study says AR tended to shift up about 40 days before major turbulent events, and that "a spike… nearly always precedes" market drops. This is in-sample and by the originators — [top1000funds](https://top1000funds.com/?p=30995)
- The only "live" test found is anecdotal. Kritzman combined AR with a turbulence (Mahalanobis) measure into a 0–100 fragility index and described one episode in which it flagged a "fragile environment" as its "first live test" — [top1000funds](https://top1000funds.com/?p=30995)
- Independent applications are supportive but in-sample. South Africa: AR rose before significant market losses — [Studies in Economics & Econometrics 2014, RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rseexx/v38y2014i2p33-46.html). Jamaica macroprudential monitoring — [MPRA 69966](https://ideas.repec.org/p/pra/mprapa/69966.html). European connectedness — [Liverpool WP](https://www.liverpool.ac.uk/media/livacuk/schoolofmanagement/research/economics/Application,Absorption,Ratio.pdf)

**Round 4. Follow-up: Is SOC/critical-point theory still defensible, given these results?**
- Sornette's school argues that near-critical signatures before 1929 and 1987 could "predict" them out of sample — [Sornette, "Predictability of catastrophic events", arXiv cond-mat/0107173](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0107173). This is LPPL-style evidence, not generic CSD, and the CSD literature above largely fails to confirm the critical-transition mechanism outside 1987.

### Inferences
- The strongest out-of-sample-ish result is a negative one: autocorrelation-based CSD fails, which weakens the literal "markets are near a critical point" story. Variance-based signals have a high hit rate but roughly 40–50% false alarms and no timing. That profile suits volatility-scaled exposure (which a vol-targeted model already does) better than discrete crash calls.
- The absorption ratio is essentially a correlation-regime indicator. Because crashes are high-correlation events, part of its "predictive" power may be contemporaneous: AR spikes as the drawdown begins. Without a published out-of-sample hit/false-alarm table it should be treated as unvalidated for timing.
- For a retail trader running a frozen, vol-targeted model: these indicators would at most serve as an overlay for gross exposure. Any such overlay would need pre-registration under the existing change-control rules. Literature evidence does not justify adding one.
