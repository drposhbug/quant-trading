# Macro-Liquidity View: Do the Fed, Money and Credit Drive Stock Returns, and Can a Retail Investor Use It?

Format: simulated expert interview. Each thread has an opening question, a sourced answer, and sharper follow-ups. Every claim is tagged **[IS]** (in-sample or descriptive) or **[OOS]** (out-of-sample, post-publication, or a pre-specified rule on unseen data). Wherever the notes say "own computation", the numbers come from scripts run on 2026-11-20 against FRED series ([WALCL](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WALCL), [WTREGEN](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WTREGEN), [RRPONTSYD](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/RRPONTSYD), [T10Y3M](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/T10Y3M)) and Yahoo ^GSPC weekly closes. The returns are price-only, with no dividends, no interest on cash and no trading costs. The scripts are `liq.py` and `liq2.py` in the session scratchpad and were not saved to the repo.

## Thread 1: Net liquidity (Fed balance sheet − TGA − RRP), M2, QE/QT and equity returns, 2008–2026

### Takeaway
"Net liquidity" fits the S&P 500 well in levels over parts of the period (r≈0.8), but that is mostly two trending series moving together. On changes the link is close to zero over the full sample, and the sign flips between regimes. Fed policy does move stocks: there is strong event-study evidence around FOMC announcements. But the best-known Fed-calendar anomalies weakened or reversed after they were published, and the academic survey finds QE effects on stocks less consistent than on bonds. A naive 4-week net-liquidity timing rule improved the Sharpe ratio in my test, but it was one of three horizons I tried, the other two failed, and its t-stat is only about 2. That is not a robust retail edge.

### Cited Findings

**Round 1. Q: What is "net liquidity" and how tight is the fit with stocks?**
- The practitioner definition is Fed total assets (WALCL) minus the Treasury General Account (WTREGEN) minus overnight reverse repo (RRPONTSYD), all from FRED. — [dharmatech gist](https://gist.github.com/dharmatech/c2dc1154167f3d1aed003aba7628a41e); [TradingView script](https://cn.tradingview.com/script/LObfHlw4/)
- [IS] One 2022 practitioner analysis reported R²=0.57 between net liquidity and the S&P 500. The same analysis said other factors dominate when the Fed is inactive and liquidity is stable. — [Advisor Perspectives 2022](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2022/08/17/s-p-3-500-by-year-end)
- [IS, own computation] Weekly data, Dec 2002 to Nov 2026, n=1,249. The level correlation is r=0.83 over the full sample. By sub-period: 2003–07 r=0.95; 2008–14 r=0.75; **2015–19 r=−0.70**; 2020–21 r=0.80; **2022–26 r=−0.48**. On *weekly changes* the full-sample correlation is **r=−0.04**, and on 4-week changes it is r=−0.24. A level fit that collapses on differences, and reverses sign by regime, is the classic signature of a spurious trend-on-trend regression. — computed from FRED [WALCL](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WALCL), [WTREGEN](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WTREGEN), [RRPONTSYD](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/RRPONTSYD)
- Current state (own computation): net liquidity is about $5.82tn (WALCL $6.74tn, TGA $0.92tn, RRP about $0). The S&P 500 is 7,176 (FRED SP500, 2026-11-19). The S&P is near highs while net liquidity sits well below its 2021–22 peak, which again shows that the level relationship broke down in the QT era. — [FRED SP500](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SP500)
- Deutsche Bank strategists said the inconsistency of the Fed-liquidity/equity correlation "reflects that it is in fact largely spurious" and attributed equity gains mainly to earnings. — [BNN Bloomberg / Deutsche Bank](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/stock-fears-tied-to-us-debt-wave-miss-the-mark-deutsche-bank-says-1.1929652)
- A practitioner study of 26-week rolling correlations found them negative in some periods and absent in others, and called the relationship "fairly random". — [Threadreader analysis](https://threadreaderapp.com/scrolly/1637041996643594240)

**Round 2. Q (sharper): Correlation isn't the point. Does a change in liquidity *lead* stocks, so I can trade it?**
- [IS, own computation] Lead correlation of 4-week net-liquidity change against the next 4-week S&P log return: full sample r=−0.03. Of 13-week change against the next 13 weeks: r=−0.12. The lead is positive only in 2020–21 (13w r=0.67) and 2022–26 (13w r=0.37). So the "liquidity leads stocks" story is a post-2020 phenomenon, not a 20-year law.
- [Quasi-OOS rule, own computation] The rule: hold the S&P 500 when the 4-week change in net liquidity is >0, using data lagged one week to avoid the Thursday H.4.1 publication look-ahead; otherwise hold cash at 0%. Results for 2003–2026: invested 59% of weeks; annualized price return 14.8% when ON vs 0.0% when OFF; strategy 8.6%/yr vs buy-and-hold 8.7%; Sharpe ratio 0.70 vs 0.54; max drawdown −40% vs −54%. Pre-2020 subsample (2003–19): Sharpe 0.58 vs 0.50. Post-2020: Sharpe 0.98 vs 0.62.
  - **Caveats:** I also tested 13- and 26-week horizons, and both *failed*. The 13-week version gave a Sharpe of 0.52 vs 0.54 for buy-and-hold over 2003–26 and 0.57 vs 0.62 over 2020–26, and the 26-week version had a Sharpe of 0.48 vs 0.55 over 2003–26. My rough t-stat for the ON-minus-OFF return gap at 4 weeks is about 2.2, before adjusting for three horizons tried. Short-horizon net-liquidity swings are dominated by TGA and RRP mechanics (tax dates, debt-ceiling episodes), so the 4-week signal may be capturing Treasury-cash calendar effects rather than "the Fed". The rule is not pre-registered, so treat it as a hypothesis, not evidence.
- [Comparison, own computation] Over the same 2007–2026 span, a plain 40-week (~200-day) moving-average trend rule on the S&P gave a Sharpe of 0.57 vs 0.47 for buy-and-hold and a max drawdown of −25% vs −54%. Over 1983–2026 it gave a Sharpe of 0.62 vs 0.55. Any liquidity-timing benefit should be compared with this free price-only filter, which does at least as well on drawdowns.

**Round 3. Q: The academic Fed literature must show the Fed moves stocks. How big, and does it persist?**
- The Fed Board survey by Knox and Vissing-Jorgensen (FEDS 2026-023) concludes "the Fed's effect on the stock market is large, even for average stock returns earned over periods of several decades". It also says "the literature on quantitative easing (QE) finds less consistent effects on stocks than on bonds". — [Knox & Vissing-Jorgensen, FEDS 2026-023](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026023pap.pdf)
- [IS] In the narrow half-hour window around FOMC announcements, rate surprises explain only R²=0.06 (Kuttner shock) to 0.26 (mps shock) of stock moves. Adding Swanson's forward-guidance and QE (LSAP) surprise factors raises this to only R²=0.31. — [same](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026023pap.pdf)
- [IS→OOS decay] **Pre-FOMC drift:** Lucca and Moench (1994–Mar 2011) found +49bp S&P excess return in the 24 hours before scheduled FOMC announcements. That is about 4%/yr, which was about 80% of the annual equity premium in their sample. In the post-publication sample (Apr 2011 to Dec 2023), what remains is about 20bp per meeting, earned overnight. During 2008–09 (15 meetings) the drift averaged about 180bp per meeting, so the QE-crisis era dominates the original result. — [same](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026023pap.pdf)
- [IS→OOS reversal] **FOMC cycle** (Cieslak, Morse and Vissing-Jorgensen): S&P excess returns in "even weeks" of the FOMC cycle were about 12bp per day higher. The survey notes "a negative FOMC cycle in much of the post-2016 sample", and the cycle was negative in 2017–2021. — [same](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026023pap.pdf)
- [IS, QE→yields] Thornton's St. Louis Fed event-study critique finds that even the evidence that QE lowered long-term *yields* is "fragile" across the 11 QE announcements. — [Thornton, St. Louis Fed WP 2013-033](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2013/2013-033.pdf)
- Ludvigson (Brookings 2022) studies QT (balance-sheet normalization) announcements using minute-level S&P data. — [BPEA FA22 Ludvigson](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/BPEA-FA22_WEB_Ludvigson.pdf). (I did not extract its point estimates.)

**Round 4. Q: What about Howell's "Capital Wars" and global liquidity?**
- Howell argues that over horizons of about 3–5 years, the main driver of annual equity returns is the change in the aggregate P/E multiple (which liquidity drives), and that earnings dominate beyond that. — [Capital Wars Substack](https://capitalwars.substack.com/p/think-correctly-about-liquidity)
- His forecasts are not free of misses. In 2023 he argued that with "95% of central banks tightening" stocks had poor risk/reward and he expected a further ~20% correction. — [Roger Montgomery summary](https://rogermontgomery.com/crossborder-capitals-liquidity-warning/). The S&P instead rose 24% in 2023. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/economy/indicator-foretold-almost-every-recession-doesnt-seem-working-anymore-rcna163522)
- CrossBorder's Global Liquidity Index is proprietary and sold as asset-allocation advice to institutions, so there is a commercial interest. I found no independent out-of-sample test of it. — [Montgomery / Montinvest](https://www.montinvest.com/investor-insights/insights/liquidity-debt-and-what-it-means-for-equities)

### Inferences
- The defensible core of the liquidity view is that Fed *policy surprises and reaction-function news* move prices and risk premia (strong event-study evidence). The popular *net-liquidity level chart* does not survive the shift from levels to changes, and it reversed sign in 2015–19 and 2022–26.
- Anomalies that are tied to the Fed calendar and publicly documented (pre-FOMC drift, FOMC cycle) shrank or reversed after publication. That is the pattern you expect from data-mined or crowded effects, and it argues against a retail investor expecting a durable edge from Fed-watching.
- Even the best version of a liquidity timing rule, if real, mainly cuts drawdowns rather than raising returns, and a 200-day trend filter does about as well. For a retail investor, the realistic use is risk management (when to be less leveraged), not alpha.

### Gaps
- **M2:** I did not run M2-versus-equity tests. The FRED M2SL series was downloaded but not analyzed. I found no peer-reviewed out-of-sample evidence that M2 growth predicts US equity returns. The 2022–23 M2 contraction, the first in decades, was followed by strong equity returns, but I have no single primary source quantifying that as a test.
- I did not extract QE-specific equity event-study magnitudes (for example Rogers, Scotti and Wright, or Swanson 2021 LSAP factor loadings on stocks).
- Proper t-stats, Newey-West adjustment and multiple-testing correction for my own rule tests were not done. The t-stat of about 2.2 is a back-of-envelope figure.
- No test was done of global liquidity (G4 central-bank balance sheets) versus US equities.

## Thread 2: Credit-cycle views (Austrian/Minsky/BIS), yield-curve inversion, HY spreads and ISM/PMI as equity regime signals

### Takeaway
Credit and curve signals forecast the *real economy* (recessions, credit losses, banking crises) far better than they forecast *stock returns*. The 2022–24 inversion was the longest in about 45 years, it produced no recession, and stocks rose more than 50%. A simple "own stocks only when the 10y–3m curve is positive" rule underperformed buy-and-hold over 1983–2026 in my test. Credit-spread signals have some out-of-sample value for short-horizon bear-market detection, and "frothy" credit (tight spreads plus high junk issuance) predicts weaker future *economic activity*. Neither is a reliable standalone retail timing tool.

### Cited Findings

**Round 1. Q: The Minsky/BIS story says credit booms end in busts. Is there out-of-sample proof?**
- [IS/OOS, crises not stocks] The BIS finds the debt-service ratio (DSR) gives "accurate early warning signals" for banking crises without detrending, and that it outperforms indicators based on credit-to-GDP even over long historical periods. — [BIS WP 1235, Drehmann, Juselius & Quincy, Dec 2024](https://bis.org/publ/work1235.htm). The original 2012 study found the DSR more accurate than the credit-to-GDP gap, which had been "the single best performing early warning indicator". — [BIS Quarterly Review Sept 2012](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt1209e.pdf)
- The credit-to-GDP gap needs at least 10 years of data before its trend estimate stabilizes, which is a real-time usability problem. — [OECD slides](https://one.oecd.org/document/COM/STD/DAF(2016)7/en/pdf)
- [IS, 1929–2013] López-Salido, Stein and Zakrajšek (QJE 2017) find that elevated credit-market sentiment (narrow credit spreads plus a high junk share of issuance) two years earlier predicts *weaker* output in the current and following year. The same approach does *not* work with stock-market sentiment measures. — [NBER w21879](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21879); [Stein PDF](https://stein.scholars.harvard.edu/resource/crdmktsentimentpdf)
- [IS] Fed FEDS paper: narrow spreads relative to history plus an elevated junk share of issuance predict reduced *corporate bond* excess returns, driven by sentiment unwinding rather than news about default. — [FEDS 2015-028](https://federalreserve.gov/econresdata/feds/2015/files/2015028r1pap.pdf)
- The Gilchrist–Zakrajšek excess bond premium (the part of credit spreads not explained by default risk) is a risk-appetite gauge. Shocks to it precede declines in consumption and activity. — [FEDS Note 2016](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econresdata/notes/feds-notes/2016/recession-risk-and-the-excess-bond-premium-20160408.html); [NBER w17021](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17021.pdf)

**Round 2. Q (sharper): Fine for the economy. Does credit predict *stock* returns out of sample?**
- [OOS] An MPRA working paper finds the default yield spread (Baa−Aaa) gives superior out-of-sample predictability of *bear markets* 1–3 months ahead among the variables it tested. — [MPRA 49093](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/49093/1/MPRA_paper_49093.pdf). This is a working paper and was not peer-reviewed in the version found.
- [OOS] In Goyal, Welch and Zafirov's re-test, the default yield spread (dfy), default return spread (dfr) and term spread (tms) are among the original 17 Goyal-Welch variables. They are *not* on the authors' list of survivors (see Thread 3). Chava, Gallmeyer and Park's loan-officer credit-standards variable (crdstd, from the Fed's Senior Loan Officer Survey, 1990–2021) was re-tested too, and it also did not make the authors' "favorites" list. — [Goyal, Welch & Zafirov slides, Mar 2023](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)
- HY OAS: FRED's public ICE BofA HY OAS series now carries only about 3 years of history (it ends at 3.52% on 2026-11-19), so I could not run a long out-of-sample test from FRED. — [FRED BAMLH0A0HYM2](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/BAMLH0A0HYM2). A spread of 3.52% is historically tight, which by the López-Salido et al. logic is a "frothy credit" reading.

**Round 3. Q: Yield-curve inversion is the most famous signal. What happened in 2022–24, and does it time stocks?**
- The 10y–3m curve was inverted from 2022-10-25 to 2024-12-13, the longest inversion in about 45 years. The 2y–10y inversion lasted about 423 days and passed 1978–80 as the longest. — [SoFi](https://www.sofi.com/article/investment-strategy/liz-looks-at-lengthening-timeframes/); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/economy/indicator-foretold-almost-every-recession-doesnt-seem-working-anymore-rcna163522)
- [OOS failure] There was no recession. BMO called the September 2024 dis-inversion a "false positive" as the economy steered a soft landing. — [BMO Economics](https://economics.bmo.com/publications/detail/9f5fe6ee-3de5-42b5-bf27-f67d7f591ebe/). The S&P rose 24% in 2023 and was about +50% from 2022-07-05 to 2024-07-23. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/economy/indicator-foretold-almost-every-recession-doesnt-seem-working-anymore-rcna163522)
- [IS, historical] Even before 2022: about three-quarters of the time stocks were up three months after an inversion, and 12-month outcomes were extreme in both directions. — [AllianceBernstein](https://www.alliancebernstein.com/us/en-us/investments/insights/investment-insights/is-inversion-panic-justified.html). Credit Suisse data showed stocks typically had about 18 months of gains after a 2s10s inversion, with recession arriving about 22 months later. — [CNBC 2019](https://www.cnbc.com/2019/08/13/after-yield-curve-inverts-stocks-typically-have-18-months-before-doom.html). Equities were positive 36 months later in 10 of 14 international inversions. — [Dimensional](https://www.dimensional.com/us-en/insights/the-flat-out-truth)
- [Rule test, own computation] Hold the S&P only when T10Y3M>0, lagged one week, otherwise cash. Over 1983–2026: invested 89% of the time, strategy 7.3%/yr vs buy-and-hold 8.9%, Sharpe 0.48 vs 0.55, max drawdown −58% vs −54%. Over 2007–2026: the S&P returned **16.1% annualized while the curve was inverted** vs 6.3% when it was positive. Being out of stocks during inversions *cost* return and did not reduce drawdown. — computed from [FRED T10Y3M](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/T10Y3M)

**Round 4. Q: ISM/PMI as a regime switch?**
- [IS] A full-sample regression of S&P returns on the ISM's distance from 50 (post-1948) has "very limited" predictive power because of large standard errors. Using it also requires forecasting the ISM 12 months ahead. — [M&G Investments 2018](https://www.mandg.com/investments/professional-investor/en-be/insights/mandg-insights/latest-insights/2018/11/beware-allure-good-looking-chart)
- [IS] ISM Services PMI level and change against next-month S&P returns, 2008–2023: r=−0.03 and −0.09. — [CXO Advisory](https://cxoadvisory.com/economic-indicators/ism-manufacturing-pmi-and-stock-returns)
- [IS backtest] Owning the S&P only when ISM >50 kept money invested 72% of the time and returned 7.3%/yr vs 8.5% for buy-and-hold, with lower drawdowns. — [Quantified Strategies](https://quantifiedstrategies.substack.com/p/ism-manufacturing-index-pmi-trading)
- [IS, descriptive] Median forward S&P returns at 1, 3 and 5 years were stronger after PMI *troughs* than after peaks (since 1950). That is contrarian, the opposite of "risk-on when PMI is strong", and peaks and troughs are identified only ex post. — [Putnam](https://www.putnam.com/dcio/content/perspectives/8239-tracking-pmi-data-and-forward-equity-returns)
- The ISM tracks S&P *earnings* growth better than price returns. — [LPL Research](https://www.lpl.com/research/blog/ism-offers-another-positive-earnings-signal.html)

### Inferences
- The consistent pattern is that macro and credit regime signals *reduce exposure during part of the bad period but miss more of the good period*. With equities' high average return, that trade-off lowers total return. The Putnam trough result and the 2007–26 "stocks did best while inverted" result suggest markets price the cycle *ahead* of the indicators.
- Credit froth (tight HY OAS plus heavy junk issuance) is the most theoretically grounded of these signals, with QJE-level evidence, but its documented target is GDP and bond returns two years out, not stock timing.
- Austrian and Minsky views are hard to test directly. Their testable proxies (credit gap, DSR) are validated for banking crises, not for equity-return prediction, and they have a 10-year trend-estimation burn-in.

### Gaps
- No long-history HY OAS (1997+) out-of-sample test. I would need ICE data beyond FRED's 3-year window, or a Baa−10y proxy (FRED BAA10Y) for a long test.
- No primary-source out-of-sample study of the excess bond premium predicting equity returns was found.
- No academic out-of-sample study of ISM/PMI predicting equity returns was found. The evidence is practitioner and in-sample only.
- The 2025–2026 outcome of the post-inversion "recession after dis-inversion" thesis is not covered by any source I found. As of 2026-11 the S&P is at 7,176, which is inconsistent with a deep bear market having occurred.

## Thread 3: The opposing camp. Macro variables predict returns poorly out of sample (Goyal-Welch 2008, GWZ 2024); which survive?

### Takeaway
Goyal and Welch (2008) found that none of 17 classic predictors (valuation ratios, term and default spreads, T-bill rate, inflation, issuance) beat the historical-mean forecast out of sample. Goyal, Welch and Zafirov (RFS 2024) re-tested 46 variables through 2021. More than a third of the newer ones lost even in-sample significance, half of the in-sample survivors failed out of sample, and the authors' summary is "Think 10% [work]. Another 10–20% with pluses and minuses. 70–80% poof." The rare survivors are mostly *not* the liquidity or credit variables the macro-liquidity camp uses.

### Cited Findings

**Round 1. Q: What exactly did Goyal-Welch show?**
- Goyal and Welch (2008): none of the 17 variables "would have helped a real-world investor outpredicting the then-prevailing historical equity premium mean". Their performance was disproportionately driven by the 1974–75 bear market. — [Goyal-Welch NBER w10483](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10483); [GWZ slides](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)
- The original 17 include variables central to the macro view: T-bill yield (tby), term spread (tms), default yield and return spreads (dfy, dfr), long-term yield (lty), inflation (infl), net equity issuance (ntis), and stock variance (svar). — [GWZ slides](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)
- Campbell and Thompson's counterpoint: with weak economic restrictions (sign constraints, no negative premium forecasts), many predictors beat the historical average out of sample. The R² is small but "economically meaningful" for a mean-variance investor. — [NBER w11468](https://nber.org/papers/w11468). Welch's reply: Campbell and Thompson defend in-sample over out-of-sample tests under a strong prior that the model is true, "IS, OOS, whatever". — [GWZ slides](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)

**Round 2. Q (sharper): The 2024 update. What survived, and is anything macro?**
- GWZ (RFS 37(11), 2024) cover 29 new variables from 26 papers plus the original 17, with data through 2021. "More than one-third… no longer [significant] even in-sample… half have poor out-of-sample performance… A small number… still perform reasonably well". — [SFI abstract](https://www.sfi.ch/en/publications/a-comprehensive-2022-look-at-the-empirical-performance-of-equity-premium-prediction); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v37y2024i11p3490-3557..html)
- The authors' **"Favorites" (survivors)**: (1) Cochrane's investment-to-capital ratio (I/K); (2) the 14 technical indicators of Neely, Rapach, Tu and Zhou; (3) short-stock interest (Rapach, Ringgenberg and Zhou); (4) fourth-quarter consumption growth (Møller and Rangvid); (5) aggregate accruals, though the authors note that only 2000–02 drove it. — [GWZ slides, Mar 2023](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)
  - Of these, only I/K and Q4 consumption growth are "macro", and both are slow annual or quarterly real-economy variables. **No monetary, liquidity, credit-spread or yield-curve variable is on the list.**
- [OOS failures of note] Martin's (QJE 2017) SVIX bound "does not outpredict, even in-sample, at statistically significant levels". Kelly and Pruitt (2013) "predicts stock returns, not equity premia", disappears when predicting returns minus inflation, and disappears when the out-of-sample period is moved earlier or later. Bekaert and Hoerova's variance premium is weak in investment terms ("better: choose opposite of vp?"). — [GWZ slides](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)
- Quick finding from GWZ: "annual variables tended to predict better" than monthly ones. That matters for retail, because it implies slow, low-turnover use, not week-by-week liquidity trading. — [same](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)
- Welch's personal conclusion: put only a "small amount of your money into timing"; "2023: I don't know what I can confidently recommend". — [same](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)
- [OOS, independent replication] A public replication and extension of GWZ in R finds only 11 of 40 predictors beat the historical average out of sample at the 10% level. This is not the original paper. — [GitHub replication](https://github.com/giacomobandinelli/equity-premium-prediction-replication)

**Round 3. Q: Isn't machine learning or combining weak signals the fix?**
- The GWZ consensus or "combo" predictor is shown in the slides as a cumulative-performance chart. I could not extract its numbers from the PDF. — [same](https://www.ivo-welch.org/research/presentations/99-stanford-prediction.pdf)
- AQR examined "Can Machines Time Markets?" (Alternative Thinking 2024 Q2). — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Alternative-Thinking/AQR-Alternative-Thinking--2Q24-Can-Machines-Time-Markets.pdf?sc_lang=en). I did not fetch it, so I have no quantified conclusions from it.

**Round 4. Q: Reconcile with Thread 1. The Fed clearly moves markets, so why doesn't liquidity predict?**
- Knox and Vissing-Jorgensen show the Fed's effect comes through *surprises* and reaction-function news, plus an equity-premium response, mostly in announcement windows or through communication between meetings. — [FEDS 2026-023](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026023pap.pdf). A *known, published* balance-sheet trajectory (QE or QT schedules, H.4.1 data) is already in prices. Unexpected changes move prices at the moment they become known, so lagged balance-sheet data has little left to predict. This is consistent with the near-zero change correlations in Thread 1 (own computation).

### Inferences
- The academic consensus directly contradicts the strong form of "the Fed and money drive markets, not earnings" as a *forecasting* claim. Policy moves prices contemporaneously, but published liquidity and credit data carry little out-of-sample information about future equity returns at retail-tradable horizons.
- What survives is mostly price-based (technical and trend indicators, short interest) or very slow real-economy data (I/K, Q4 consumption). For a retail investor, the most transferable survivor is the trend or technical filter: cheap, observable, and in my test it matched or beat any macro rule on drawdown control.
- **Retail usability verdict:** macro-liquidity is useful as a *narrative and risk-awareness* lens (for example: QT plus TGA rebuilds plus tight HY spreads mean be cautious with leverage). It is not useful as a timing signal expected to beat buy-and-hold after costs and taxes. The strongest out-of-sample evidence says any timing should be a small slice of the portfolio (Welch's own advice) and should prefer slow or annual signals or trend filters over weekly liquidity prints.
